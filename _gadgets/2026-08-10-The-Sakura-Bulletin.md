## Chapter One — The Room at the End of the Hall

### April

The cherry trees along the **lane** to school were full of **blossom**. Every year it is the same. The flowers open, everybody takes photographs, and eight days later a **breeze** blows them all away.

I walked up the hill at six in the morning. That is not normal for a student. But I do not **commute** by train like the others. My family owns a bakery, so my day **commences** early. At four in the morning my father turns on the **oven**. At five my mother pours **flour** into the machine. At half past five I carry the first **loaf** of bread to the front of the shop and put it on a **tray**.

Our shop is small and **modest**. Two tables, six chairs, a paper **napkin** holder, and a shelf of **cereal** boxes for people who want breakfast. My mother is the **cashier**. Every **client** who comes in is a neighbour, so a **purchase** takes twenty minutes, because nobody can buy bread without telling my mother a story first.

"Sota," my father said. "Bread."

He never uses more than two words **beforehand**. He waits until the bread is finished, and then he says "bread."

I put the last **loaf** on the **rack**, wiped my hands on my **sleeve**, and left.

---

My name is Sota Aoki. I am seventeen. I go to Kitami High School, a **decent** public school near the sea, with a **gymnasium** that leaks when it rains and an **auditorium** with three broken seats in the back row.

I am a second-year student, and I belong to the newspaper club.

That sounds better than it is. I *am* the newspaper club. All of it.

The club room is at the end of the third-floor hall, past the science room, in a corner where nobody walks. Inside there is one long table, four **stools**, a metal **cabinet** full of old papers, and a **lamp** with a **bulb** that flickers. Somebody left a **mop** in the corner in 1998 and nobody has moved it since.

The room is a little **gloomy**, and the window is small, but it is mine.

I unlocked the door and turned on the light. **Dust** floated in the air. On the table was my writing **pad**, my pen, and the last issue of our paper, printed on cheap grey paper.

The name of the paper is *The Sakura Bulletin*.

My grandfather started it forty years ago. Back then, the club had eighteen members. There was a **columnist** for sports, a **columnist** for food, and a girl who wrote a weekly **commentary** about the school lunch that was so cruel the **headmaster** asked her to stop.

Now there is me.

I sat down and wrote a **heading** on the empty page: *Spring Term Begins*. Then I looked at it. Then I put a full stop, then a **comma**, then a **colon**, then I crossed all three out.

Writing a **headline** is the hardest part of **journalism**. Everything after it is easy.

---

At **midday** the door opened and Ms. Hayashi came in, holding a cup of cold coffee. She is our English teacher and our club advisor. She is thirty-five, tired, and kind in a **peculiar** way — she never says anything nice directly.

"Aoki," she said. "Bad news. Sit down."

"I am sitting down."

"Then sit down harder."

She put a piece of paper on the table. It was an official **memorandum** from the school office, with a **clerical** stamp in the corner and the **secretary**'s signature under it. I read it twice. The words were **literal** and short, and they did not care about me at all.

> *NOTICE: A club with fewer than five (5) members shall **cease** to exist. Any club that does not meet this rule by the first day of the November school festival will face **dismissal** from the club list, and its room will be returned to the school.*

I read the last line a third time. My **wrist** felt stiff. I put down my pen.

"Five members," I said. "By November."

"Five," Ms. Hayashi said. "Including you."

"That is not a rule. That is a death sentence with a date on it."

"It is an **ongoing** problem for every small club. The judo club has three people and one of them is a **dropout** who comes back only for lunch." She drank her cold coffee and made a face. "The student council will **proceed** with a check every month. Kurosawa is handling it."

"Of course he is."

Ryo Kurosawa is the vice president of the student council. He is tall, **punctual**, and always **stiff**, like a man wearing a shirt one size too small. He believes in rules the way other people believe in gods.

"Aoki." Ms. Hayashi tapped the paper. "Do not look so **dull** about it. Four people. You have seven months. Go and find four human beings who like words."

"In this school?"

"In this world," she said, and left.

---

I did not go home at the usual time. I stayed and cleaned the room, because cleaning is what I do when I am worried. I picked up the **litter** under the table. I found a 1993 **bulletin**, a broken **comb**, and one sock. I do not want to know.

At five o'clock I went down to the notice board by the shoe lockers and pinned up a small **sketch** I had drawn: a newspaper with legs, running. Under it I wrote:

**NEWSPAPER CLUB — WE NEED FOUR PEOPLE. NO EXPERIENCE NEEDED. WE HAVE A ROOM AND A KETTLE.**

It was not a great advertisement. But at the **outset** of anything, you only need one person to stop and read.

I sat on the stairs across the **aisle** and **awaited** my future.

Nobody came for forty minutes.

Then somebody came.

---

She stopped in front of the board and stood there with her hands behind her back. She was in our school uniform, but the **collar** of her white **blouse** was folded differently — the Tokyo way, I think — and her shoes were too clean. New shoes. New student.

She read my poster from the top to the bottom. Then she read it again. Nobody reads a poster twice. That is not **commonplace**. That is not part of anybody's **routine**.

She lifted one hand and touched the paper, very lightly, near the word *WORDS*.

I stood up too fast and hit my **elbow** on the wall.

She turned around.

Her eyebrows went up — first one, then the other, which is a thing I have only ever seen her do. There was a **faint** mark of blue ink on her thumb.

"Is this yours?" she asked.

"Yes. Sorry. The drawing is bad. I know the legs are wrong."

"The legs are fine." She looked back at the poster. "The **conjunction** is wrong. 'We have a room *and* a kettle' sounds like you are proud of the kettle."

I opened my mouth and nothing came out.

"Use a **preposition**," she said. "'A room with a kettle in it.' Then the kettle sounds like a joke, and people like jokes."

"...Who are you?"

"Mizuki Hoshino." She gave a small, **stiff** bow. It was the bow of somebody who has **enrolled** at six different schools and has stopped expecting anything from the seventh. "I transferred here yesterday. Class 2-B."

"I'm in 2-B."

"I know. You sat by the window and drew a newspaper with legs for the whole of maths."

My face went hot. "That is a **genuine** accusation."

"It is a **genuine** fact." She almost smiled. Almost. Her mouth moved about one millimetre.

Then she looked at the poster one more time, and something in her face closed, like a door pushed shut by a **breeze**.

"Good luck," she said. "I don't write."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You were going to."

She was right. I was.

She walked away down the hall, and her shoes made no sound at all, and I stood there rubbing my **elbow** and feeling like the world's least **punctual** person — the kind who thinks of the right sentence four hours late.

---

That night I sat in the **attic** above the bakery, where my grandfather kept his old boxes. I turned on the **lamp**. The **bulb** buzzed.

Inside the boxes were forty years of *The Sakura Bulletin*. Grey paper, soft as cloth. On the front page of the very first issue, in 1985, my grandfather had written a short line under the **heading**, as a kind of promise:

*A school is a thousand people who never say what happened to them. This paper is here to ask.*

I sat on the floor for a long time.

Then I took my writing **pad** and wrote a list of four names. Three of the lines were empty.

On the first line I wrote: **Mizuki Hoshino** *(says she does not write. This is a lie.)*

Downstairs, the **stove** clicked. The **breeze** came in through the window and smelled of the sea and of bread.

Seven months.

I could work with seven months.

## Chapter Two — Four Empty Lines

### Late April

To **recruit** four people, you need two things: **publicity**, and something worth joining.

I had neither. So I decided to make the second one first.

"If I print one **genuine** article," I told Ms. Hayashi, "one that people actually want to read, then the members will come."

"That is a **daring** plan," she said. "It is also the plan of every failing newspaper in human history."

"Do you have a better one?"

"**Bait**," she said. "Free food."

She was joking. She was also completely right, but I did not know that yet.

---

I began with the baseball team, because I had an **ally** there.

Kenji Mori has been my friend since we were six. He is loud, he is **boastful**, and he can eat four bowls of rice without stopping to breathe. He is also the second-best pitcher in our school, and he never lets anybody forget it.

I found him behind the **gymnasium**, in the batting **cage**, hitting balls with a **vigorous** and slightly angry **swing**.

"Aoki!" He did not stop. "Come to write about me at last?"

"I've come to **inquire** about the team."

"That's the same thing." He hit another ball. His **calf** muscles were shaking. "Ask your questions."

I took out my writing **pad**. "How is the **workout** going?"

"Terrible. Coach made us run to the harbour and back. Kondo threw up in a **hedge**." He **swung** again and missed, and made a sound like a dying animal. "Also there's a **scout** coming in June. From a university. He's coming to look at Hayama, not me."

There it was. I stopped writing and looked at him.

"Are you **envious**?"

"Of course I'm **envious**." He put down the bat. He was breathing hard, and his **grip** on the fence made his knuckles white. "Hayama is better than me. Everybody knows it. I've known it since we were twelve. But I'm the one who comes at six in the morning. I'm the one who does the extra hundred. And nobody writes that down anywhere."

For about ten seconds, neither of us spoke.

Then I said, quietly: "I'll write it down."

Kenji looked at me. He rubbed the back of his neck. "...That's stupid."

"Yes."

"Do I get food?"

"There is bread at my house."

"Then I'm in the club." He picked up the bat again. "I'll take the photographs. I have no **aptitude** for words, but I have a phone and I have **guts**."

"You have to come to meetings."

"I'll come when there's bread."

That is not a **pledge** anybody would call strong. But I wrote his name on the second line, and I felt the room in my chest get bigger.

Two of five.

---

The next day I made **publicity**.

I printed forty posters at the school office, which cost me all my coins, down to the last **penny** of my lunch money. I put them in the hall, in the **aisle** by the shoe lockers, and on the door of the **auditorium**.

Then I stood at the school gate at seven in the morning and gave them out, one by one, to everybody who walked past.

It was not a **competent** performance. My hands shook. My voice came out too high. A third-year student took a poster, looked at it, folded it into a paper plane, and threw it at his **comrade**. Somebody in the **brass** band **corps** shouted "Good luck, newspaper guy!" and blew two loud notes on his **trumpet**, and everybody laughed, and about four people **clapped**.

Four people, and no **applause** after it. Just the small dry sound of four pairs of hands, and then silence.

I stood there and thought: *I could stop now. Nobody would even notice.*

Then I gave out the next poster.

---

At lunch, somebody sat down opposite me without asking.

"Aoki Sota. Class 2-B. Newspaper club, one member." Yuka Tanabe put her hands flat on the desk. She is our class representative. She talks like a train that has decided not to use the brakes. "I have a **proposal** for you."

"I'm eating."

"You can eat and listen. That is one of the great **facilities** of the human body."

She is like this. You do not fight it. You wait.

"In July," she said, "there is the student council election. I am going to be a **candidate**. To win, I need **publicity**. You need readers. So we **cooperate**. I give you interviews, you give me space in the paper. Total **cooperation**. A **cooperative** relationship."

"You want me to write about you."

"I want you to write about the election. Fairly." She looked at me very seriously. "If you write only nice things about me, nobody will believe the paper, and then your paper is worth nothing, and then my **publicity** is worth nothing. I am not stupid, Aoki. I **reckon** you will be tougher on me than anyone."

I put down my chopsticks. I had, at the **outset**, thought of Yuka Tanabe as a **distraction** — a loud girl who liked being in charge. I had been **erroneous** about that. I had **erred** completely.

"Are you joining the club?" I asked.

"No. I'm going to be too busy." She stood up. "But I'll be your best **respondent**. Ask me anything and I'll answer within an hour. That's a promise."

"Why?"

She stopped. For one moment the train slowed down.

"Because last year I wanted to read something honest about this school," she said, "and there wasn't anything. There was only **gossip**."

---

That week I wrote my first real article.

It was about Kenji. About the boy who is second-best and comes anyway. I called it *The Hundred Extra Swings*. I worked on it for four nights in the club room, under the flickering **bulb**, with cold tea and no company.

Writing it was harder than I expected. Every time I got near the true thing, my sentences went soft, as if they wanted to **hedge**, to protect everybody. I had to **strive** against my own politeness. Three times I nearly gave up and wrote something safe and **dull** instead.

On Thursday night I finished the draft. It was bad. I knew it was bad. The **bulk** of it was fine, but the middle part had no **cohesion** — the parts did not hold together — and the ending simply stopped, like a train running out of track.

I left it on the table, turned off the **lamp**, and went home to sleep.

---

On Friday morning, the draft was still on the table.

But it was covered in red ink.

Somebody had gone through the whole thing, line by line. Not cruelly. Carefully. In the margin next to my weak middle section: *You are explaining. Show the thing instead.* Next to a sentence I was proud of: *This is a nice sentence, but it is doing nothing. Cut it.* Next to my ending: *Do not stop. Land.*

And at the top, in the same small, straight handwriting:

*The person who does the extra hundred is the story. The scout is not the story. Stop looking at the scout.*

There was no name. There did not need to be one. I had seen the **faint** blue ink on that thumb.

I sat down slowly on a **stool**.

Then I turned the last page over, took my pen, and wrote in the empty space at the bottom:

*Thank you. Meeting is Monday at four. There is a kettle in the room. Also bread.*

I did not lock the door that night.

---

On Monday at four o'clock, I sat in the club room and **awaited** her.

At five past four, the door opened.

Mizuki Hoshino stood there with her bag against her chest, like a shield. She did not come in.

"I'm not joining," she said.

"Okay."

"I only fixed it because it was wrong and it was going to be printed. That's all. A wrong thing being printed is worse than a wrong thing sitting in a drawer."

"Okay."

She stayed in the doorway. The silence went on so long that it stopped being silence and became something else.

"...Is there really bread?" she said.

I pushed the paper bag across the table.

She looked at it. She looked at the **mop** in the corner, and the 1993 **bulletin**, and the metal **cabinet**, and the small window with the **breeze** coming through it.

Then Mizuki Hoshino took three steps into the room and sat down on the **stool** across from me, still holding her bag, and said, with the **bearing** of somebody signing a very serious international agreement:

"One piece. And I am not joining."

She ate two.

## Chapter Three — The Season of Rain

### June

In June it rains in Japan for a month without a serious pause. We call it the rainy season. Everything becomes **damp**. Your shoes never dry. The air in the classroom is **moist** and warm, and by the third week even the walls smell like a wet **rug**.

On the first Monday of June, water began to come through the ceiling of the club room.

It came in one thin line, near the metal **cabinet**, and it fell into a small **puddle** on the floor. I put a **bucket** under it. Then, on Tuesday, a second line started, so I put a plastic **tray** under that one. By Thursday we had four containers on the floor and the room played music all afternoon: *tick, tick, tock, tick*.

"The **plumbing** upstairs is old," said Mr. Doi, the school caretaker, who came up to look and then did nothing about it for four months. "Above you is the old **laundry** room. The pipes there are beginning to **decay**."

"Can you fix them?"

"I can put it on a list." He looked at the wet ceiling with real affection, the way other men look at old cars. "Nothing here is **waterproof**, boy. This building has been slowly falling apart since before you were born."

After he left, Kenji said, "This room is going to **disintegrate**."

"It is not going to **disintegrate**."

"It is going to fall into small pieces and I will be inside it."

Mizuki, who was sitting in the corner with a book and officially not a member of anything, said without looking up:

"The **disintegration** of a building takes about eighty years. You will be fine."

Kenji stared at her. Then he stared at me. Then he mouthed the words *she talks?* with his eyebrows so high they nearly left his face.

---

She had come almost every day since April, and she had not joined once.

That was our arrangement. She sat in the corner. She read. Sometimes she looked at my drafts and made small marks in red ink and gave them back without comment. When I asked her a direct question about writing she answered with **restraint**, in short sentences, as if each word cost her money.

I did not push. Something in her had a lid on it, and I had a feeling that if I pulled at the **lid** she would simply stop coming.

But I noticed things, because that is my **pastime**, and also my problem.

I noticed that when Kenji said something funny she looked at the floor to hide her face.

I noticed that when a first-year student put his head around the door and asked if this was the newspaper club, she went completely still, like an animal that has heard a sound.

And I noticed the notebook. Small, black, soft at the corners. She carried it everywhere and never once opened it in front of us.

---

On the last Friday in June, the rain came down so hard that the school **halted** all club activity and sent everybody home.

I found Mizuki standing under the roof by the gate, looking out at the water, not moving.

"No umbrella?"

"It was sunny this morning."

"It has not been sunny in nineteen days."

"It was sunny in my heart," she said flatly, "and then reality **interfered**."

I laughed. It **startled** her — I saw her shoulders jump — and then she looked away, and I saw the corner of her mouth do the millimetre thing again.

I opened my umbrella. It is a cheap plastic one from the bakery, and it is not big.

"Which way do you live?"

"...Down the hill. Past the **garage** with the blue door."

"That is my way."

It was not my way.

---

We walked. The rain **splashed** up from the road onto our shoes and our socks and our legs, and in about ninety seconds we were both **soaked** anyway, which made the umbrella into a joke that neither of us mentioned.

Our shoulders touched. Then they did not. Then they touched again. Neither of us said anything about that either.

Halfway down the hill, past a house with a wet **balcony** and a **chimney** with no smoke, she said:

"Why do you do it?"

"Do what?"

"The paper. You have two members. You are going to lose the room in November. You know that."

"I know that."

"Then why?"

The rain got louder for a moment and then quieter, the way it does.

"My grandfather started it," I said. "But that's not the real answer. The real answer is worse." I moved the umbrella a little more over her side. "I think there are about a thousand people in that school and almost none of them ever say anything true out loud. And I think if nobody writes it down, then in three years it will be like none of it happened. Kenji's hundred extra swings. Tanabe standing at the gate at seven in the morning. All of it just... gone."

She was quiet.

"That is a very **earnest** thing to say," she said at last.

"Yeah. Sorry."

"I did not say it was bad."

We reached the bottom of the hill. There is a bus stop there with a small roof and one bench, and we stopped under it, because the rain was too heavy to keep going.

Water ran down the road in a small river. Her hair was flat and wet against her forehead. There was a **haze** over the whole town, so thick that the sea and the sky had become one grey thing.

And then, without any warning at all, she said:

"I wrote something once, and a person got hurt."

---

I did not speak. I have learned this from a year of interviews: when somebody finally opens the door, do not walk through it. Stand still.

"At my old school in Tokyo," she said. "I was on the paper there too. In my first year." She looked straight ahead at the rain. "There was a teacher who was taking money from the club budget. Not a lot. Small amounts, for two years. I found out. I checked it four times. Then I wrote it, and it was true, and they printed it."

"That's good work."

"He had a daughter in the second year." Her voice did not change. That was the frightening part. "The story went outside the school. It was on the news for two days. He lost his job. His family had to move. And his daughter, who had done nothing, had to leave in the middle of the year, with everybody watching her walk out of the gate."

The rain hit the roof above us.

"Everything I wrote was true," Mizuki said. "Every fact was correct. And I still think about her every single day."

"Did they blame you?"

"Some of them. The **suspicion** was that I did it to be famous." She gave a small sound that was not a laugh. "There was an **accusation** in a letter. Somebody **alleged** that I had been paid. It was not true. But **rumour** does not need to be true, it only needs to be repeated."

"So what happened?"

"The school made me stand in front of the whole year group and read an apology I did not write." She finally turned and looked at me. Her eyes were dry, which was somehow worse. "I read it. Then I went home. Then I stopped writing. That was a year and a half ago."

I thought about all the things a person could say. *It wasn't your fault. You did the right thing. You were brave.* Every one of them was true and every one of them was useless, and she had heard all of them already.

So instead I said:

"Do you still think it was worth printing?"

She was silent for a long time.

"Yes," she said. "That's the part I can't get rid of. If it happened again tomorrow, I would print it again." She pressed her hands together. "And that makes me feel like a person with something wrong inside her."

"Or a person with a **conscience** that doesn't switch off."

"Those are the same thing, Aoki."

The rain began to get quieter.

"Is that why you won't join?"

"I won't join because I am not going to do that to another person," she said. "And because I don't trust myself to stop once I start."

She stood up and stepped out from under the roof into the rain, and turned around to look at me, standing there getting wet, with the whole grey town behind her.

"Also," she said, "your club room is going to fall down."

"It is *not* going to fall down!"

"Goodnight, Aoki."

She walked away into the water.

I stood at the bus stop for another five minutes with a useless umbrella in my hand, feeling the **damp** in my socks and something much worse than **damp** somewhere under my ribs.

Then I walked home and wrote in my notebook, on the page with the four lines:

*She does not need me to tell her she is good. She knows. She needs someone to sit in the room with her while she decides what to do about it.*

---

That night the rain finally stopped.

In the morning there was **dew** on every leaf in the garden, and the sun came up over a town that had been washed completely clean, and the **puddle** in the road outside the bakery held a perfect small copy of the sky.

## Chapter Four — The Election

### July

By July the rain had gone and the heat had arrived. The cicadas started at six every morning and did not stop. The school put one electric fan in each classroom, and every fan turned its head slowly from side to side, like a bored teacher.

And on the notice board, a white sheet went up with a red **heading**:

**STUDENT COUNCIL ELECTION — JULY 15**

Two students had put their names forward. There would be a real **contest**.

---

Our school has a small **constitution**. It is four pages long, it was written in 1974, and almost nobody has read it. I have read it three times, because I am that kind of person.

It says the student council is chosen by a secret **ballot** of all students. It says the council controls the club budget. And in Article 9 it says that the **headmaster** keeps the right to **veto** any decision the council makes — which he has used exactly twice in fifty years, both times about food.

The **charter** for clubs is a separate document. It has eleven **clauses**. **Clause** 6 is the one that was going to kill me: *five members or **dismissal***.

**Clause** 6 was two years old.

**Clause** 6 had been written by Ryo Kurosawa.

---

The first **candidate** was Yuka Tanabe. Her **candidacy** surprised nobody. She had been preparing since she was born.

The second was Kurosawa himself.

I went to the council room on Monday to arrange interviews. The room is on the second floor and it is the only room in the school with a working air conditioner, which tells you something about **politics** everywhere in the world.

Kurosawa was at the desk, writing. He did not look up.

"Aoki. Newspaper club. Two members."

"Three, on a good day."

"Two." He put down his pen and folded his hands. Everything about him was **stiff** and clean and exactly straight. "I know why you are here. You want an interview so that you can **contradict** me in print."

"I want an interview so that I can print what you say."

"Those are usually the same thing."

"Only if what you say is bad."

Something moved behind his eyes. It was not anger. It was closer to interest, which was worse.

"Sit down," he said.

I sat.

"You think I am your enemy," Kurosawa said. "That is **conventional** and it is lazy. Let me give you facts. This school has a budget. It is not large. The **gymnasium** roof leaks in four places. The science lab has one **facility** for washing eyes, and it is broken. There are twenty-two clubs. Nine of them have fewer than five members, and together those nine take eleven per cent of the money."

"So you wrote **Clause** 6."

"I wrote **Clause** 6." He said it without any **pretence**, which I respected and hated at the same time. "A club is people. Two people do not **constitute** a club; they **constitute** two people with a key. If there are no people, it is a room with a kettle in it. I am not going to **conceal** my position to be popular, Aoki. I am not a politician."

"You are literally running for election."

"That is different," he said, and for one second — one — the corner of his mouth moved, and I understood that Ryo Kurosawa had a sense of humour buried somewhere under all that ironing.

Then it was gone.

"You may have your interview," he said. "Print it in full. Do not cut it to make me sound worse. If you do, I will **reject** the paper's budget in September, and I will be **entitled** to do it."

"And if I print it fairly?"

"Then we will still be on opposite sides," he said. "But we will be on opposite sides honestly."

---

That week, Ms. Hayashi's English class read a newspaper article from America, because she said we should see how other people argue.

It was about health care. It had the word **Medicare** in the first line, and the word **federal** in the second, and by the third line half the class had given up.

She wrote the hard words on the board in **chalk**.

"A **congressman**," she said, "is a member of the American **Congress**. The **Congress** has two parts. One of them is called the **Senate**. In Britain they call it **Parliament** instead, and the leader is a Prime **Minister**. In some countries the leader is called the **Premier**. In a **republic**, the head of the country is elected. Yes, Mori, this will be in the exam."

Kenji put his head on his desk and made a small dying sound.

"A **liberal** person," Ms. Hayashi went on, "usually wants change. A **conservative** person usually wants to keep things as they are. Neither of these is a bad word, whatever your uncle says at New Year. A **moderate** person stands in the middle. A **neutral** person takes no side at all."

"Which are you, sensei?" somebody asked.

"I am a teacher," she said. "I am **neutral** in public and terrible in private."

At the back of the room, Mizuki was writing all of it down. Every word. She had the look on her face that I had started to recognise: the look of somebody preparing for a fight.

---

The election special took nine days.

We did it properly. I interviewed both **candidates** for an hour each, and typed every word. Kenji photographed them. Yuka answered every question within the hour, exactly as she had promised, which made her the best **respondent** I have ever had.

And Mizuki — who was still not a member — checked the facts.

She did it at night, in the club room, with the door open so that it did not officially count as being in a club.

"Kurosawa says nine clubs take eleven per cent," she said. "Where did that number come from?"

"He said it in the interview."

"That is not where a number comes from. That is where a number *arrives*." She held out her hand. "Get me the budget papers. The real ones. From the school office."

It took me two days and a great deal of politeness to the office **secretary**. When I brought them back, Mizuki spread the pages across the table and went through them line by line with a ruler, the way a person defuses a bomb.

At eleven o'clock she said: "It is nine point six per cent, not eleven. He has included the **brass** band, which has thirty-one members. He is not lying. He is being generous with himself."

"Should we print the real number?"

She looked up at me.

"You should print both," she said. "His number, and then the number from the papers, and then let the reader decide. That is not an attack. That is a **contrast**."

I wrote it down exactly like that.

Then I said, carefully, without looking at her: "You know that this is the best thing in the whole issue."

"I know."

"It has your work in it."

"It does not have my name on it," she said, and closed the file, and that was the end of the conversation.

---

The **preliminary** debate was on July 13th in the **auditorium**, in front of six hundred sweating students.

Yuka spoke first. She was excellent. She talked about **welfare** for students, about a place to go when things at home were bad, about the small clubs as the only room in the school where a strange kid can breathe. Some of it was too soft. All of it was **genuine**.

Kurosawa spoke second, and he was terrible in a way I had not expected — he was correct. Numbers, dates, the state of the **gymnasium** roof, the cost of repairs, a plan with three stages. It was **contentious** and cold and completely honest, and by the end even the students who hated him had gone quiet.

Then the floor opened for questions, and I stood up, because that is my job.

"Aoki Sota, *Sakura Bulletin*. A question for both **candidates**." Six hundred faces turned. My legs stopped feeling like legs. "**Clause** 6 removes a club when it has fewer than five members. My question is not about money. It is this: what is a school *for*? If your answer is 'to be efficient', say so, and I will print it, and everybody here will know. Tanabe first."

I did not sit down.

Yuka said: "A school is for the people in it. Even the awkward ones. Especially them."

Kurosawa was quiet for three seconds, and then he said:

"A school is a promise to be here next year. Things that cannot keep that promise should end with **dignity**, so that the things that can, survive." He looked directly at me. "I am sorry, Aoki. That is my real answer."

The **auditorium** was completely silent.

Then, from somewhere in the middle rows, somebody started to **clap** — one person — and then it spread, and the **applause** was for both of them, and I sat down and my hands were shaking and I thought: *this is the best thing I will ever print.*

---

The **poll** was two days later.

We printed our election special that morning, four pages, five hundred copies, cheap grey paper. Kenji and I gave them out at the gate at seven o'clock. By eight-thirty they were gone. I found people reading them in the corridor, in the **aisle**, on the stairs, in the **lavatory** queue. Somebody in the third year had cut out the budget **contrast** and pinned it to the notice board.

Five hundred copies. Gone.

The result was **proclaimed** at four o'clock.

**Kurosawa: 289. Tanabe: 271.**

Eighteen votes.

I found Yuka afterwards, sitting alone in the empty classroom, very upright, with her hands in her lap. She had not cried and she was not going to.

"Eighteen," she said.

"Eighteen."

"Do you know what makes me angry? I don't think he's wrong about the money." She laughed once. "I think he's wrong about people. But I don't have a number for people."

"Nobody does. That's why we write it down instead."

She looked up at me then.

"Your paper was fair," she said. "You printed the thing that helped him. The budget answer. I read it and I thought, *that is going to cost me votes*, and it did, and it was still the right thing to print."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," Yuka Tanabe said. "Be like that in November, when it's your turn."

---

That night the club room was hot and quiet. Kenji had gone to training. I sat by the open window with the **bulletin** on the table and the cicadas outside.

Mizuki came in without knocking, put her bag down, and stood there.

"Two hundred and eighty-nine to two hundred and seventy-one," she said.

"Yeah."

"That paper moved votes, Aoki. Both ways. That is a real thing." She sat down opposite me. She looked at her own hands for a while. "I read every page four times."

"And?"

"And I want my name on the next one," said Mizuki Hoshino.

I did not say anything clever. I got out my notebook, and I opened it to the page with the four lines, and I crossed out the note in brackets next to her name — *(says she does not write. This is a lie.)* — and I wrote instead: **MEMBER.**

Three of five.

Outside, somewhere down the hill, somebody let off a firework, three weeks too early.

## Chapter Five — Three Days in August

### August

Every club in Japan has a summer camp. The tennis club goes to the mountains. The **brass** band goes to a hall and plays the same eight bars for four days. It is a national law, I think.

The newspaper club had never had one, because for two years the newspaper club had been one person, and one person going away alone is not a camp, it is a problem.

This year we had three members and one loud friend of the club.

"Where are we going?" Kenji asked.

"My grandmother's farm."

"That's not a camp. That's a family visit."

"It is two hours away, near the sea, and there is a summer festival on Saturday."

Kenji thought about this for one second. "It's a camp," he said.

---

We took the train, then a small local line, then a **tram** that ran along the coast with the windows open. The **fare** was three hundred and ten yen. Yuka Tanabe came too. I had asked her out of politeness, expecting a polite no, and instead she had said "Yes. I need to be somewhere that is not this school," in a voice that ended the discussion.

The **tram** went past the **outskirts** of a small town, then past nothing, then past a wide green valley, and everybody stopped talking and looked out of the window.

Rice fields. **Paddy** after **paddy**, flat and shining, for what looked like a hundred **acres**. A grey heron standing in one of them. A **flock** of small birds turning all together like one animal.

"I have never seen this," Mizuki said quietly.

"Rice?"

"Space."

---

My grandmother was waiting at the tiny station in a straw hat.

She is seventy-nine. My grandfather died eleven years ago, so she is a **widow**, and she has run the farm alone since then, which she describes as "easier, because he talked too much." She is small and brown and has hands like tree roots.

She looked at the four of us. Then she looked at Mizuki. Then she looked at me. Then she smiled in a way I did not like at all.

"So," she said. "**Kin** and **folk** and a girl."

"Grandmother."

"I said nothing." She picked up Mizuki's bag before anybody could stop her. "You are all here for three days. You will eat, you will work, and you will not say the word 'phone signal' in my house, because there is none. Come."

The farm is at the end of a dirt road, past a **marsh** where the frogs live, past an old **mill** that stopped turning in 1962. There is the main house, a **barn**, a wooden **hut** for the tools, and a **cellar** under the kitchen where she keeps things cold.

Kenji looked around at the whole valley and said, with total sincerity: "This is the most **hospitable** place I have ever seen and I have not even eaten yet."

---

### Day One: Work

"You said we would work," Yuka said at six the next morning. "I did not think you meant *work*."

"You are on a farm," said my grandmother. "What did you think happened here?"

She gave us jobs. Kenji went to **plough** a small field with the old **tractor**, which he described afterwards as "the greatest day of my life." Yuka pulled **weeds** for four hours and did it with the same terrifying energy she brings to everything. I loaded boxes onto a **wagon**, then onto a small **lorry**, which then took them to a shop in town.

Mizuki was sent to the **dairy** shed.

I found her there at nine o'clock, standing at a safe distance from a **calf**, taking notes.

"It licked me," she said.

"They do that."

"It licked me with **vigour**." She wrote something down. "There is also a **bull** in the next field. Your grandmother says he is 'friendly'. Aoki, that animal is the size of a car."

"Are you writing an article about a cow?"

"I am writing an article about food," she said. "Nobody in our school knows where anything comes from. I did not know. There is a **fertile** field, and somebody puts **fertilizer** on it, and somebody **sows** the seed in spring, and five months later they **reap** it, and then it is on your desk in a plastic packet and you think it came from a shop." She looked at the **calf**. "That is a story."

She was right, and it became the best piece in our October issue.

At **midday** we sat on the grass and ate rice balls, and Kenji told a story about the **tractor** that was ninety per cent invention, and Yuka laughed for the first time since the election, a real laugh with no politics in it.

In the afternoon I helped my grandmother in the kitchen. She was **grinding** something in a stone bowl.

She coughed twice while she did it. It was a dry cough, and it went on a little too long.

"How long have you had that?"

"Since June."

"Have you seen a doctor?"

"I have an appointment in September," she said. "It is nothing. Stop looking at me with your grandfather's face."

I let it go.

I have thought about that afternoon a great many times since.

---

### Day Two: The Sea

The sea is four kilometres from the farm, down a road between **pine** trees.

The town there is a working **harbour**. Not a beach town — there is no **cruise** ship, no hotel, no shop selling ice cream in the shape of a **fairy**. There is a stone **dock**, a small **fleet** of fishing boats, a **ferry** that goes to an island twice a day, and a rusting **steamer** that has not moved since the 1980s and is now, officially, a **monument**.

We walked out along the **dock**. The boats were tied up at their **anchorage**, moving slowly with the water. On the nearest **vessel**, two men of the **crew** were fixing a net on the **deck**, and one of them waved at us.

"Can we go on the **ferry**?" Kenji asked.

"It costs money and it goes to an island with nothing on it," I said.

"Perfect."

We did not go. Instead we climbed the **cliff** path at the north end, above the **gulf**, where you can see the whole curve of the coast.

At the top there is a small **memorial** stone for eleven fishermen who died in a storm in 1954. The names are cut into it. Under the names there is one line: *The sea gives and the sea takes.*

Mizuki read it twice.

"Your grandmother said something this morning," she said. "She said the **tide** here can **drown** a strong man in one metre of water. She made me repeat it back to her."

"She does that to everyone. She did it to me until I was fifteen."

"Why?"

"Because she lost people here," I said. "So she says it out loud, to everybody, every time. It is the only kind of worrying she allows herself."

Mizuki was quiet, looking down at the water going in and out over the rocks.

"I like your grandmother," she said. "She is not gentle and she is not cruel. She just tells you the true thing and then lets you get on with it."

Below us, the **surf** came in and went out again. Somewhere behind, Kenji shouted something about a crab.

"That's what you do," I said. "In red ink."

She did not answer for a moment.

"Aoki," she said. "Don't be nice to me on a **cliff**. It is dangerous."

---

### Day Three: The Festival

The summer festival is held in the shrine grounds on the hill above the town.

It starts at seven, when the sky goes orange. There is a **procession** first — the men of the town carrying the portable shrine on their shoulders, shouting in time, moving down the main street in a slow **parade**. Old women stand at the front of the crowd. Children sit on their fathers' shoulders. Everybody follows.

At the shrine, people **worship**. You throw a coin, you **clap** twice, you **pray**. Kenji **prayed** for a university **scout**. Yuka **prayed** for something she refused to say. I do not know what Mizuki **prayed** for, because I did not look, because that would have been cheating.

Then the **lanterns** come on, and the whole hill turns gold, and the festival begins properly.

There were forty stalls along the path. Grilled **pork** on sticks with a dark sweet **sauce**. **Sausages**. Corn **roasted** black at the edges. Sweet potato. Shaved ice in six colours. A **magician** doing card tricks badly for a crowd of eight-year-olds who had already worked out how he did it. A man dressed as a **clown**, handing out balloons for a **charity** collection. A stall selling small wooden **souvenirs**, made by a **carpenter** from the town.

Kenji ate for one hour without a serious pause. Yuka won a goldfish and immediately began planning its future.

And Mizuki came up the hill late, because she had been getting changed, and she was wearing a blue summer **gown** with white flowers on it — a *yukata* — which her mother had made her bring "in case," and which she clearly had not expected to wear.

I have thought about what I said at that moment about four thousand times since. What I said was:

"Oh."

That was it. That was the whole sentence.

"Say something else," Mizuki said. "Quickly. Please."

"You look —" I stopped. Everything I could think of was too big. "It suits you."

"That is a terrible sentence."

"I know. I'm a writer, not a talker."

"You are barely a writer," she said, and her ears were red, and she walked past me very fast into the crowd.

---

At half past nine, the fireworks started.

Everybody went to the field behind the shrine to watch. We lost Kenji to a **chorus** of drunk uncles who had adopted him. Yuka went to protect the goldfish from the crowd.

Mizuki and I ended up at the top of the field, on the grass, above everyone else.

The first one went up with a sound you feel in your chest, and then broke open, and for a second the whole hill was white — six hundred faces all turned the same way, all looking up, everybody in the town at once.

"Aoki."

"Yeah."

"Thank you for bringing me here."

"It's just a farm."

"It is not just a farm." Another firework opened, red this time, and I could see her face lit up in it. "I have moved six times. Every time, I learn the train lines and the shop names and I do not learn anybody's grandmother." She was looking straight ahead. "This is the first place in three years where somebody said *come with us* and meant a real place, with a **cellar** in it, and a **bull**, and a stone with names on it."

I did not say anything.

"So. Thank you."

She turned to look at me at the same moment that I turned to look at her, and the distance between us was very small, and getting smaller, and the sky was going gold and then green and then gold again, and neither of us was breathing—

"AOKI!"

Kenji, forty metres down the hill, standing on a bench, with a bag of food in each hand and no idea in the world what he had just done.

"AOKI! THEY HAVE THE ONES WITH THE **SAUCE**!"

Mizuki put her face in her hands.

I lay back on the grass and stared up at the exploding sky and thought about all the things I would say to Kenji Mori over the next fifty years.

"...We should go down," Mizuki said, into her hands.

"Yeah."

"Aoki."

"Yeah?"

"Not never," she said, and stood up, and walked down the hill.

I sat on the grass for another ten seconds with my heart going like a **tractor** engine.

*Not never.*

I could work with *not never*.

## Chapter Six — Two Letters in September

### September

Two letters arrived in the same week. I have never forgiven September for that.

---

The first came to the bakery on a Tuesday.

My mother read it standing at the **reception** desk of our own shop, in her apron, with a **tray** of bread going cold beside her. Then she sat down on a chair, which she never does during working hours.

My grandmother had gone to the hospital for the cough. They had found a **tumour** in her lung. It was small. It was probably not the bad kind. They would do more tests in October and take a **specimen** to be sure.

"Probably," my mother said. "The doctor said 'probably'."

I read the letter four times, looking for a word that would fix it. There was no such word. There was a **medicinal** name I could not pronounce, a date, a hospital **ward** number, and a line about her heart rate and her **pulse** being "good for her age."

"She is seventy-nine," my father said, from the door. Four words. A record.

"She's alone out there," I said. "The nearest shop is six kilometres. She drives a **lorry** from 1988."

"She will not move," my mother said. "I have asked her eleven times. She says the farm is her **livelihood** and her home and she is not going to sit in a small flat in a town and wait."

She folded the letter and put it in her apron **pocket**, and then she stood up and went back to selling bread, because that is what my family does with feelings: we put them in a **pocket** and we go back to work.

That night I looked up the word online for two hours. I learned about **germs** and cells and what a **tumour** actually is, and none of it helped, because the **incidence** of a thing in a population tells you nothing at all about one small brown woman in a straw hat.

---

The second letter was pinned to the club room door on Thursday. It was printed on council paper, and it was **conclusive** and cold and perfectly correct.

> **FORMAL NOTICE OF DISCIPLINARY REVIEW**
>
> The Newspaper Club (3 members) is below the minimum set out in **Clause** 6 of the Club **Charter**. A **disciplinary** review will be held on 20 September at 16:00 in the council room. The club may send one **delegate** to speak. The council is not **obliged** to grant any **extension**.
>
> *R. Kurosawa, President*

Kenji read it over my shoulder. "He can't do that."

"He can. He wrote the rule and now he is the rule."

"It's September! The deadline is November!"

"It's a *review*," I said. "It is not the end. It is somebody checking that the end is on schedule."

Mizuki took the paper out of my hand, read it once, and said: "'Not **obliged** to grant any **extension**.' That sentence is doing a lot of work. He is telling us the answer before we ask the question."

"So we don't go?"

"We go," she said. "But we do not go and beg. We go with paper."

---

We had eight days. We used all of them.

Mizuki ran it like a small war. She made a list on the board and would not let anybody leave until their part was done. Kenji, who has never in his life been organised, printed two hundred photographs and sorted them by date because he was **frightened** of her.

We built a file. Every issue we had printed that year. The number of copies taken at the gate, counted by hand. A **poll** of one hundred and twelve students, done in one lunch break, with the questions written down so nobody could say we had pushed the answers. Yuka Tanabe delivered thirty of those forms herself and told me she was doing it "as a private citizen", which was the most political sentence I have ever heard.

Ms. Hayashi looked at the file on the last evening and drank her cold coffee.

"You know I can come as your **counsellor**," she said. "A teacher is allowed to speak."

"No."

"Aoki."

"If a teacher speaks for us, then we're children asking an adult for help." I closed the file. "We have to be **accountable** for ourselves. Otherwise **Clause** 6 is right about us."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she said, in a completely different voice: "Your grandfather said something like that once. I was in his class."

"You knew him?"

"Everybody knew him," she said. "He was **indispensable** and he was extremely annoying. Good luck, Aoki."

---

On the sixteenth, Mizuki did not come to school.

She did not come on the seventeenth either. On the eighteenth I asked the class **secretary**, and she said, without looking up: "Hoshino? **Influenza**. There's a small **outbreak** in the first year."

I did a stupid thing. I finished school, I went to the shop, I filled a bag, and I walked down the hill past the **garage** with the blue door until I found the building with her name on it.

Her father opened the door. He is a tall, tired man who works for a **corporation** that moves him every eighteen months. He looked at me the way fathers look at boys with bags.

"...Aoki, from the club," I said. "I brought food. I'll go now."

"She has been asking about the review for three days," he said, and stepped back. "Come in. Ten minutes. And do not go near her, the doctor was **emphatic**."

---

She was on a futon in the small room, wrapped in a blanket up to her chin, with a **thermometer** on the **mat** beside her and her hair completely wild.

"Go away," she said. "I look terrible."

"You look ill."

"Those are the same thing." She coughed. It was a horrible sound. "What are you carrying?"

"Bread. **Toast** is easier when you can't eat. And soup. My mother says you have to **nourish** yourself or you'll be weak for a month."

"Your mother has never met me."

"She has now, in theory."

I put the bag down and sat by the door, which was as close as I had been told to come. Her face was pale and there was a red mark on her cheek from the pillow. On the low table there was a **packet** of white **tablets** the doctor had **prescribed**, and a glass of water she had clearly not touched.

"Take the medicine."

"It is **overdue**," she agreed, and did not move.

"Hoshino."

"Fine." She took one, made a face, and drank a small **sip**. "There. Are you happy."

"Deliriously."

She lay back and looked at the ceiling. I did not **dare** say anything about how ill she looked, because she would have thrown the **tablets** at me. Outside, somebody's radio was playing **jazz**, badly, through a wall.

"The review is on Friday," she said.

"You are not going to the review."

"I am going to the review."

"You have **influenza**. You will breathe on the school president and I will be charged with a crime."

"Aoki." She turned her head. "You are going to walk into that room alone and be polite and be reasonable, and he is going to beat you, because he is better at being reasonable than anybody in this school." She was breathing carefully, in the way sick people do. "You need somebody in there who is not polite."

I did not answer straight away.

"There's something else," I said. "My grandmother's ill."

The room got very quiet.

I told her all of it: the **tumour**, the tests in October, the word "probably", my mother's apron **pocket**. It took about ninety seconds. I had not said any of it out loud to anybody, and it turned out the words were all just sitting there behind my teeth, waiting.

When I finished, Mizuki did not say *she will be fine*. She did not say *I'm sure it's nothing*.

She said: "That woman told me about the **tide** twice, in one morning, so that I would not **drown**."

"...Yeah."

"Then she is a person who repeats the important thing until it sticks." Mizuki pulled the blanket up. "So repeat it to her. Every week. Phone her every Sunday and be **conscientious** about it, even when there is nothing to say. That is the whole job."

I looked at the floor for a while.

"I'll do the review," I said. "Rest."

"I'll be there."

"Hoshino—"

"Aoki," she said, "there are two things happening to you at once and you are trying to carry both of them by yourself, which is **arrogant**, and also stupid, and also very like you." She closed her eyes. "Bring the file tomorrow. I will read it lying down."

At the door, her father gave me a very small nod, which I have decided to treat as a **certificate** of good character.

---

### September 20th

The council room at four o'clock. The air conditioner was on. Kurosawa sat in the middle with two council members on either side, like a small **jury** who had already decided.

I put the file on the table.

"You may speak," he said.

I spoke for eleven minutes.

I did not beg. I gave numbers: five hundred copies of the election special, gone by half past eight. One hundred and twelve students in the **poll**; seventy-one said they had read the paper that term; forty said they had changed their mind about something because of it. I gave the article about the farm. I gave the piece about Kenji. I put down the photographs.

Then I said the last part, which Mizuki had written out for me on a page of her notebook, in her small straight handwriting, and which I had refused to change:

"**Clause** 6 counts members. It does not count readers. A club of thirty people who do nothing and a club of three people who print five hundred copies are not the same object, and any rule that cannot see the difference is not a strict rule. It is a **superficial** one."

Kurosawa did not move.

Then the door opened.

Mizuki Hoshino came in wearing an **overcoat** over a jumper over pyjamas, with a **germ** mask on her face and a temperature of thirty-eight point one, carrying a second file, and she put it down on the table with a **thud** that made both council members jump.

"Hoshino," said Kurosawa. "You are unwell."

"I am **contagious**," she said, "so please be quick." She opened the file. "**Clause** 6 has a **loophole** and you have all missed it for two years."

The room went completely still.

"**Clause** 11 says that any club may **contend** that it serves a school function, and that a club serving a school function may apply for **advisory** status and continue with three members, subject to review." She turned the page around so they could read it. "It is on page four. It was written in 1974 and it has never been removed. And I would like to formally **propose**, on behalf of the newspaper club, that a school newspaper is a school function."

Nobody spoke for about six seconds.

Then one of the council members leaned over and read the page, and went slightly grey, and whispered something to Kurosawa.

Kurosawa read it himself. Twice.

"...This is correct," he said.

"I know," said Mizuki.

"It is a **loophole**."

"It is a **clause**," she said. "You like **clauses**."

And Ryo Kurosawa — I will remember this until I am eighty — Ryo Kurosawa put his hands flat on the table, and looked at the two of us, and made a small sound that was almost certainly a laugh being **strangled** at birth.

"The review is **postponed** to November," he said. "**Advisory** status is granted for October. The rule stands. Get her out of my council room before she **poisons** everyone in it."

---

In the corridor, Mizuki leaned against the wall and slid down it and sat on the floor.

"I feel," she said, "extremely unwell."

"You were magnificent."

"I know." She pulled the mask down. Her face was grey. "Aoki, you have to get me home. I used all of my strength on the word 'loophole'."

I got her a **cab**. It cost more than I had, so Kenji paid the rest, and complained about it for six months, and never once let me pay him back.

That Sunday I phoned my grandmother.

There was nothing to say, so I told her about the **calf**, and the **cab**, and the girl with the mask.

She laughed until she coughed. Then she said: "Every Sunday, Sota."

"Every Sunday," I said.

## Chapter Seven — Everything at Once

### October

October at a Japanese high school is not a month. It is a machine that eats sleep.

The school festival is on the first weekend of November. Every class must run something. Every club must show something. And for four weeks the whole building smells of paint and glue and hot glue guns and the particular anger of teenagers holding a hammer for the first time.

Our class, 2-B, voted for a café. Yuka Tanabe ran the vote, counted it twice, and **proclaimed** the result like a general.

Class 2-A chose a **circus** theme. Class 2-C chose a 1930s **gangster** bar, which the teachers approved without reading properly, and which included two plastic **pistols** and a fake **rifle** on the wall until Friday, when a teacher finally read it properly.

The drama club chose a historical play.

And the newspaper club chose to do the impossible.

---

### The Machine

"We will print a festival special," I said, on the first Monday. "Sixteen pages."

Kenji stopped eating. That is how you know something is serious.

"Sixteen," Mizuki said.

"Sixteen. A guide to every class, every club, every stall. Photographs of everything. A **commentary** on the play. An interview with the president. A map."

"Aoki," Mizuki said slowly, "our longest issue this year was four pages and it nearly killed you."

"I know."

"You want four times that, in four weeks, with three people, while class 2-B builds a café."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I had thought about how to say this for two days and had not found a clever way, so I said the plain one.

"Because on the twentieth of November, Kurosawa's review happens, and if we are just a club that survived on a **loophole**, we will lose. We have to be something the school would actually miss."

Nobody spoke.

Then Kenji cracked his knuckles and said, "Right. I'll need a better camera and a great deal of food," and Mizuki opened her notebook to a clean page and wrote **16** at the top, and underlined it twice, and that was the whole discussion.

---

### The Café

Building a café inside a classroom is a **chore** that no one describes honestly afterwards.

We had four days, a budget of eleven thousand yen, and the enthusiasm of thirty-two people with no **knowhow** whatsoever.

Yuka **coordinated**. She had a plan on the board with times on it, and she stood in front of it like a **marshal** directing an army.

We built a counter out of **timber** from the woodwork room, held together with fifty **screws**, four **bolts**, and a quantity of glue that a **carpenter** would find offensive. Kenji, holding a **spade** for reasons nobody could explain, was made to carry things instead. Somebody spilled a bag of **cement** in the corridor, and it stayed there until March.

We covered the tables with white cloth and painted a **stripe** of blue along the wall, and then a second **stripe**, because the first one was **clumsy** and bent. We hung two paper lamps on a **cord** from the ceiling and ran an extension **cable** along the floor, taped down, because the **utility** points in that room date from 1974 and there are only two of them.

Ms. Hayashi came to look at the electrics, holding her cold coffee.

"If this building burns down," she said, "I will say I saw nothing."

The menu was my department, because of the bakery. We would sell **loaves**, small cakes, and coffee. My father, who says four words a month, said eight words about this: "Bring the trays back. All of them. Clean."

At home, in the evenings, I helped him bake extra **stock** for the festival. We filled the **freezer**. We filled the **refrigerator**. We filled a neighbour's **refrigerator**. My mother worked out the money on paper, like an **accountant**, listing every **ounce** of flour and every **litre** of milk, because a school café that loses money is a school café that gets closed.

"You need to sell four hundred and twenty items to break even," she said. "After that, everything is profit for the class fund."

"How do you know that so fast?"

"I have been the **cashier** of a small business for twenty-six years, Sota," she said. "Money is not difficult. It is only tiring."

---

### The Play

The drama club's play was called *The Last Winter of Lord Sanada*.

It was a historical drama, set four hundred years ago, and it was — I say this with love — extremely long. There was a castle under **siege**. There was a **noble** family. There was a **maiden** who was secretly the daughter of the enemy. There was a scene in which a **warrior** made a speech about **loyalty** for four and a half minutes without breathing.

I went to two rehearsals to write my **commentary**, and I took Mizuki, because she said she wanted to see "what a **legendary** disaster looks like from the inside."

It was not a disaster. That was the surprise.

The **armament** on stage was made of painted wood and cardboard, and the "**battalion**" was six third-year boys walking in a circle to look like sixty. The **imperial** flag was a bedsheet. And still, in the scene where the **conqueror** finally comes through the gate — the **climax** of the whole thing — the club president stood in the middle of the stage in a **disguise** made of curtain fabric and delivered a line about **conquest** and **exile** that made the back of my neck go cold.

"Oh," Mizuki said quietly, beside me in the dark.

"Yeah."

"They are actually good."

"Yeah."

She wrote something in her notebook.

Later, in the prop store behind the stage, we found: eleven wooden swords, a broken **torch**, a box of hats, a stuffed **crocodile** from a 1998 production nobody could explain, and a real **typewriter** from about 1955.

Mizuki picked up the **typewriter** with both hands like it was a baby.

"Aoki."

"No."

"Aoki, they are going to throw this away."

"Hoshino, it weighs fourteen kilos."

She carried it to the club room herself. It took her nine minutes and three rests. It is still there. It works. She has never once used it for anything, and she would fight a **bull** for it.

---

### The Online Edition

The **typewriter** is funny because of what we did that same week, which was the opposite.

The idea was Kenji's, which nobody believes when I tell them.

"Five hundred copies," he said, with his mouth full. "That's the whole print run. But my cousin's school puts theirs online and the old students read it. In America. From their houses."

"We do not have a website."

"The school has a website."

"The school website was made in 2009 and it has a picture of a **fax** machine on it."

But we asked. And it turned out the school website was maintained by exactly one person: a first-year student named Sasaki, who had been quietly running the entire school's digital **infrastructure** since April because nobody had ever asked who was doing it.

Sasaki listened to my whole plan without speaking, then said: "You want to **computerize** the archive too? Forty years of it?"

"...Could we?"

"Yes." He pushed his glasses up. "It is not difficult. It is only long. Bring me the paper and I will scan it."

He built us a page in one weekend. He set up an **outlet** for each issue, with a **specification** he wrote himself so that every file would look the same — he used the word **standardise** eleven times in one conversation. He explained about **variables** and file sizes for six minutes, and my brain went out of the window and looked at some trees.

"There is one thing," he said at the end. "Anything you put in **cyberspace** is public. Forever. A person who wants to **hack** a school website does not even need to be a **hacker**, because the password is the school's founding year. I have told them four times."

"Should we be worried?"

"Probably not," said Sasaki. "Nobody attacks a school newspaper."

I have thought about that sentence a lot since.

---

### The Sunday Calls

Every Sunday at seven, I phoned my grandmother.

The first call was terrible. Neither of us knew how to do it. We got through four minutes and both said goodbye with relief.

The second was better. The fourth was easy.

By the fifth Sunday, she had opinions about all of them. She wanted to know whether the girl with the mask had recovered. She wanted to know if Kenji had got his university **scout** (he had not; the **scout** had come and looked at Hayama and left, and Kenji had gone to the batting **cage** and hit balls until his hands bled, and I had written none of it because he asked me not to). She wanted to know how much we were charging for coffee, and told me it was too cheap, and she was right.

In the middle of the sixth call she said, casually, as though it were nothing:

"They did the second test. It is not the bad kind."

I had to sit down on the stairs.

"Sota?"

"I'm here."

"They will take it out in January. It is a small operation. I will be in the **ward** for four days and then I will be **mended** and extremely annoyed about the cost."

"Grandmother."

"Yes?"

"I'm—" There was no word. The **relief** had taken all of them. "That's good."

"It is good," she agreed. "So stop holding your breath and go and build your café. And bring the girl again in the spring, when the **blossom** is out. It is better then."

---

### The Last Night Before

On the thirty-first of October, at ten past nine, the three of us were the last people in the school.

The classroom was finished. The counter was up. The **stripes** were painted. The lamps hung on their **cord**. Four hundred and twenty items were in three **refrigerators** across the town.

And in the club room, on the table, the sixteen pages were laid out in order, printed, checked, and checked again — the map, the class guide, the interview with Kurosawa (printed in full, exactly as promised), Mizuki's piece about the farm, Kenji's photographs, the **commentary** on the play.

Mizuki was asleep sitting up, with her head against the **cabinet**, still holding a red pen.

Kenji was asleep on the floor.

I stood in the middle of the room with the flickering **bulb** above me and looked at it: sixteen pages, three people, one **loophole**, four weeks.

I remember thinking, with absolute certainty, that this was the top of the year. That everything from here was going to be easy.

That is the last completely happy thought I had for six weeks.

## Chapter Eight — The Second Paper

### November 1st — The Good Day

I want to write this day down carefully, because of what happened to it afterwards.

The gates opened at nine. By ten past nine there were people everywhere — students, families, small children, grandparents, old students back from university with that slightly **superior** look they all get after six months away.

Our café sold out of cake by half past eleven.

Yuka Tanabe ran the floor like a **banquet** manager. She had put a paper **purse** under the counter for the coins and made two people responsible for the **currency**, because, she said, "money disappears in a crowd, and I am not going to **allege** anything about anybody afterwards." A local **merchant** who supplies our bakery came in and bought four coffees and told my father, in front of me, that the bread was good. My father said, "Yes," and went red to the ears.

And the *Sakura Bulletin* festival special — sixteen pages, three hundred copies to start — was gone by noon.

We printed two hundred more. Those went in ninety minutes.

I stood at the top of the main stairs at two o'clock and watched a woman I had never met sit on a step with the map open on her knees, telling her husband which classroom to go to next.

Kenji came past with a camera round his neck and a bag of food and said, "Aoki, you look like you're about to cry."

"I'm not about to cry."

"You're a **coward** and a liar," Kenji said, kindly, and gave me a rice ball.

At four o'clock, in the corridor outside the drama club's room, an old woman stopped Mizuki and asked whether she was the one who had written the piece about the farm. She said she had grown up on one. She said nobody writes about that. She held Mizuki's hand for about five seconds.

When she had gone, Mizuki stood completely still in the middle of the corridor.

"Hoshino?"

"Give me a minute," she said, in a very odd voice, and turned to face the wall.

I gave her a minute.

That was the good day. It lasted eleven hours.

---

### November 2nd — The Bad One

The second paper appeared overnight.

Somebody had put it in the shoe lockers. All of them. Four hundred and ten lockers, between eleven at night and six in the morning.

It was four pages. It was printed on the same cheap grey paper we use. It had our **heading** at the top, in our font, with our masthead and our forty-year-old line underneath, because every issue of the *Sakura Bulletin* since 1985 was now sitting in **cyberspace** where anybody in the world could copy it.

It was a **forgery**. And it was **contemptible**.

Page one **alleged** that Ms. Hayashi was being removed from her job in March for "**negligence**", with a fake quote from an unnamed "**informant**" in the school office.

Page two said that the election in July had been decided by **bribery** — that Yuka Tanabe's family had paid for the posters and that a council member had acted as a **broker**.

Page three was about a first-year girl. I am not going to write what it said. It was a **rumour** with a name attached to it, and it was cruel in the specific, careful way that only somebody who knows a school can be cruel.

Page four was a list. Fourteen names, under the **heading** *PEOPLE WHO THINK THEY ARE BETTER THAN YOU*.

I read all four pages standing at my own locker at half past seven in the morning, and I could feel my heart going, and my hands were cold, and the thing I remember most clearly is that some part of my brain was saying, stupidly, over and over: *the margins are wrong, the margins are wrong, we never use margins that wide.*

---

By eight o'clock, the school had **split** in two.

Half of it knew immediately that it was fake. The other half did not, because the other half had never thought about how a newspaper is made and had no reason to.

A crowd formed outside our club room. It was not a **riot**. It was worse than that: it was thirty people standing very quietly, waiting to see what my face would do.

Kenji got there before me and stood in the doorway with his arms out like a **bouncer**, which is the single bravest thing I have ever seen him do.

"It's not ours," he was saying. "It's not ours. Go away. It's not ours."

Somebody at the back said, loudly: "Then who's it from?"

---

The headmaster's office. Ten o'clock.

Present: the headmaster, the year head, Ms. Hayashi, Kurosawa as council president, and me.

Ms. Hayashi had read page one. She was completely calm and she had put her coffee down, which frightened me more than shouting would have.

"For the record," she said, "I am not being removed in March."

"Nobody thinks you are," said the headmaster.

"Eleven parents have already read it," she said. "By Friday it will be forty. That is how this works. You do not **negate** a thing by saying it is untrue. It just sits there."

The year head put the four pages on the desk in front of me.

"Aoki. Is this your club's work?"

"No."

"Do you know who made it?"

"No."

"Could somebody get your design? Your typeface, your masthead?"

"Yes." My voice came out flat. "In October we put forty years of the paper online. Anybody in the world can download an issue and copy the top of the page. It took us three weeks to build. It would take a person about twenty minutes to steal."

The headmaster rubbed his eyes.

"Then whoever did this," he said, "used a door that you opened."

I did not have an answer for that. I still do not, really.

---

And then Kurosawa spoke, for the first time.

"Sir. There is something the committee should be aware of."

He put a printed page on the desk. It was a news article from a Tokyo paper, from eighteen months earlier. There was a photograph of a school gate. There were the words *school newspaper*, and *teacher dismissed*, and *first-year student*.

I felt the room tip sideways.

"A member of the newspaper club," Kurosawa said, "was involved in a similar incident at her previous school. I am not **alleging** that she did this. I am saying that it will come out within a day, and that this committee should not be the last people in the building to know it. If we look like we are hiding something, we lose the school."

"Where did you get that?" I said.

"An anonymous letter. In my locker. This morning." He met my eyes without flinching. "The same night as the papers."

The year head turned to me.

"Aoki. Did you know about this?"

And here it is. The two seconds.

I want to be honest about what happened in my head, because I have had a long time to look at it.

I was seventeen. I had slept nine hours in three days. I had five hundred copies of the best thing I have ever made sitting in a cupboard, and a **forgery** with my grandfather's masthead on it lying on the headmaster's desk, and a first-year girl I had never spoken to who was, at that moment, at home, and a teacher whose career had just been damaged for no reason at all.

And I was **frightened**.

So I said: "...No. I didn't know."

Three words. Two seconds.

The door was open about ten centimetres, because the year head had not closed it properly when he came in.

---

She was not waiting outside for me afterwards. I looked in the club room, the corridor, the library, the stairs at the back by the science room.

I found her at the shoe lockers, putting her indoor shoes away, with her coat already on.

"Hoshino."

"Don't."

"Let me—"

"I heard it, Aoki." She did not turn around. "You didn't have to lie for me. That is not the part I mind. Lying for me would have been stupid but I would have understood it."

"Then what—"

"You said it like it was a **stain**." She closed the locker. "You said *I didn't know* the way a person says *it wasn't me*."

"That's not what I—"

"I know," she said, and finally turned, and her face was completely dry and completely still, which by now I knew was the worst thing her face could do. "I know that is not what you meant. That is the problem. That is what it sounds like when the truth arrives at the wrong speed, and I have heard it before, from an entire hall of people, and I promised myself I would never stand in front of it twice."

The corridor was full of people not looking at us, which is a specific kind of noise.

"I'm resigning from the club," she said.

"Hoshino, don't. You're a third of the club. The review is on the twentieth—"

"I *know* when the review is." Her voice **split**, just once, on the word *know*, and then went flat again. "I know exactly what my name is going to do to that review. In twelve days a man with a **charter** in his hand is going to ask whether the newspaper club is safe, and if I am standing there, the answer is no, whatever anybody says, because I am a **rumour** with legs."

"I don't care about the review."

"You have cared about the review since April!" she shouted. It was the only time. "You built sixteen pages for the review! Don't insult me by pretending you can drop it in one afternoon because a girl is upset!"

Nobody in that corridor moved.

"I'm going to find out who did it," I said.

"Good. Do that." She picked up her bag. "And Aoki — I did not do this."

"I know that. I never for one second—"

"I know," she said. "But you should have said it in the room."

She walked out through the gate.

---

### The Twelve Days

I will not describe all of them.

The **setback** was not one event. It came in pieces, every day, in the ordinary way that bad weeks actually happen.

I should say something here about what four pages of **deceit** actually do to a building with a thousand people in it, because I did not understand it before November and I have never been able to explain it properly since.

It is not **rage**. There was some **rage** in the first two days — a **raging**, useless kind, mostly from third-years who had nothing to do with any of it — and it burned out by the weekend. It is not **grief** either, although there was some of that.

What it actually is, is a **gloom** that gets into everything. A general **arousal** of feeling that nobody can turn off and nobody can aim at anything useful. People **glanced** at each other in corridors. Conversations stopped when the wrong person walked in. Three separate friendships in my year ended that fortnight over things that had nothing to do with the paper, because once you have been **provoked** into suspecting one thing, you start **searching** everywhere.

And underneath it, in me, there was the thing I did not admit to anybody until now: a small, **lame**, **dumb** part of my brain that kept **clinging** to the idea that if I just found the right sentence, all of it would go away in a morning. That is not hope. That is **cowardice** wearing hope's coat. Every night I would sit down to write the sentence, and every night my stomach would **sink**, and I would go to bed at one o'clock having **scarcely** written forty words.

I did not fall into **despair**, exactly. **Despair** is a clean word and this was not clean. It was closer to being **deprived** of something — the **deprivation** you feel when a room you liked has stopped being safe.

The first-year girl on page three did not come to school for eleven days. Her mother came in on the Tuesday. I saw her walk past the staff room, and I had to go and stand in the **lavatory** for a while.

The **rumour** about Ms. Hayashi reached her old university friend, who phoned her about it, which is how she learned that it had left the building.

Yuka Tanabe was interviewed twice about the **bribery** page, entirely without cause, and she answered every question with perfect politeness, and then she came to the club room and sat down and said, "Aoki, if you find him, I want ten minutes alone with him first," and she was not smiling.

Ryo Kurosawa was **blameless** and everybody hated him anyway, because he had put the Tokyo article on the table and somebody had to be blamed for something.

And the **online** archive came down. Sasaki took it offline himself on the Monday, without being asked, and sent me one message: *I am sorry. I should have foreseen this. The password was the founding year.* A first-year student, apologising to me, for a thing that was not his fault, at eleven o'clock at night.

We went to two members. The room was very quiet. Kenji came every single day, ate, said something stupid, and stayed until I left, and he never once mentioned that he was doing it. I do not know how I would have **coped** without him, and I have never told him so.

On the eleventh night I sat in the club room with the **bulb** flickering and the **typewriter** on the **cabinet** and the four pages of the **forgery** spread out on the table in front of me.

I had read them about two hundred times by then. Not to feel bad. Looking.

Because here is the thing that had been scratching at the back of my head since the first morning, underneath everything else:

*Page four. The list of fourteen names.*

Eleven of them were students.

Three of them were not.

Three of them had left this school two and a half years ago — and there was no way for anybody currently enrolled to know why those three names belonged together.

I sat up very slowly.

Then I went to the metal **cabinet**, and I opened the bottom drawer, where forty years of the *Sakura Bulletin* sit in date order, and I started pulling out everything printed between April and December, three years ago.

## Chapter Nine — What Happened Three Years Ago

### November 13th

The bottom drawer of the metal **cabinet** smells of old paper and dust and 1997.

I sat on the floor with the issues from three years ago spread around me in a half circle, and I worked backwards from December, and at half past one in the morning I found it.

It was a small box at the bottom right of page three. It had run in every issue for eight months. It was called **THE WHISPER PAGE**.

It was a gossip column.

I read all eight months of it in one sitting, and by the end I felt physically sick.

It was clever. That was the horrible part. Whoever wrote it never printed anything that could be proved false, because it never quite said anything. It used the shape of a question. *Which second-year is spending a lot of time in the staff room?* *Which club president's marks went up rather suddenly?* No names. Just enough detail that four hundred people could work out the name in under a minute.

And in the June issue, three years ago:

> *Which second-year girl came into this school with a score nobody in her middle school believed? Some people are asking how. We are only asking why nobody else is asking.*

That was all. Thirty-one words.

I looked at the bottom of the column for the writer's name, and there it was, in small letters, the way the club has always done it.

**K. Sudo (3rd year)**

Then I got out the September issue, and the column was gone, and in its place was a short notice from the editor saying that *The Whisper Page* had ended and that the club "regrets the distress caused."

Nine words of apology for eight months of work.

And on page four of the **forgery** — the list of fourteen names — three of the names were the three teachers who had signed that notice.

---

### November 14th

I found Kurosawa in the council room at seven in the morning. He is always there at seven.

I put the June issue from three years ago on the desk in front of him, folded open to page three.

He looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"Where did you get this?"

"Bottom drawer. We keep everything." I sat down without being asked. "The girl in that column. What was her name?"

"Kurosawa Aya," he said.

"Your sister."

"My sister."

He said it in exactly the voice he uses for budget figures, which is how I knew.

"She was second year. I was in the last year of middle school, so I was not even in this building yet." He turned the paper around very carefully with one finger, so that it faced away from him. "Her score was real. She had studied for two years. She was the sort of person who is **conscientious** about everything, including things nobody asked her to do."

"What happened?"

"Nothing happened. That is what people never understand." He folded his hands. "Nobody attacked her. Nobody wrote anything else. Thirty-one words, once, in June. And then for four months people were slightly quieter when she came into a room."

He stopped.

"She stopped eating properly in October. She stopped coming in November. She finished her schooling by post and she never went to university, and she is twenty-one now and she works from home and she is fine, and she has not been inside a school building since she was sixteen."

The air conditioner hummed.

"I wrote a complaint," he said. "I was fourteen. It was eleven pages long and it was extremely badly written and I delivered it to this school by hand, and three teachers signed a notice that ended the column, and the third-year who wrote it was removed from the club in his final term. His name was Sudo Kaname."

"He's on page four of the fake paper," I said. "Along with those three teachers."

"Yes," said Kurosawa. "I noticed that on the second of November."

"You *noticed*—"

"I noticed it while I was putting a Tokyo news article on the headmaster's desk," he said, and for the first time since I had known him, his voice was not level. "Do you want to know why I did that, Aoki? Because I have spent three years watching a school **overlook** a thing that was written down in its own building, and I decided when I was fourteen that I would never again be a **bystander** who kept quiet to be kind."

"So you told them about Hoshino."

"I told them a fact that was going to arrive anyway. I chose the speed at which it arrived." He looked at his hands. "I was correct, and it was **injurious**, and both of those are true at once, and I have not slept properly since."

Neither of us spoke for a while.

"**Clause** 6," I said. "You wrote it because of her."

"Yes."

"You wrote a rule to close a club, slowly, legally, over years, so that nobody would ever have to say out loud what it was for."

"Yes," said Ryo Kurosawa. "That is exactly what I did. It is the most **cowardly** thing I have ever done and it took two years and eleven meetings and everybody thought it was about money."

He stood up and went to the window.

"Aoki. I am going to say something now and I would like you not to be pleased about it."

"Okay."

"For three months I have been reading your paper," he said. "The election special. The piece about your friend and his hundred swings. The farm. That is not *The Whisper Page*. That is the opposite of it. And I have known that since July and I did not change the rule, because changing the rule would have meant admitting that I built it out of my sister."

He turned around.

"Find Sudo," he said. "I will help you. Whatever you need — council authority, the office, the archive, my name on a request. Take it."

---

### The Week

We were four, in the end, though only two of us were officially members.

Kenji did the legs. Mizuki was not there, and I felt her absence in the room like a missing tooth.

Sasaki gave us the first solid thing. He had never actually deleted the server records, because Sasaki does not delete anything.

"One address downloaded the entire archive on the twenty-eighth of October," he said. "Forty years, in nineteen minutes, at two in the morning. Everybody else who has ever used the site read one issue at a time. That is not a reader. That is a **seizure** of the whole thing."

"Can you tell who?"

"No. I can tell you it was not from inside the school, and I can tell you it was the same connection twice more that week." He pushed his glasses up. "Also, you should know: whoever it was looked at June, three years ago, first."

The second thing was paper.

Our grey paper is not normal. It is a cheap recycled stock and there is exactly one shop in this town that still sells it: Ito Stationery, by the station, where the newspaper club has bought its paper since 1985 because the owner gives us a **discount** out of loyalty to my grandfather.

Kenji went in and asked Mr. Ito whether anybody had bought a large quantity recently.

Mr. Ito, who is eighty-one and **overheard** everything in that town for sixty years, said: "Yes. A young man. About twenty. Two hundred sheets, end of October. He was rude about the price."

"Did you know him?"

"He used to come in when he was at your school," said Mr. Ito. "He was rude then too."

---

We did not go to the police. We did not need to. This was not a matter for a **court**, and nobody was going to **gaol**, and if I am honest I did not want an **attorney** anywhere near it. I wanted to look at him.

Kurosawa found the address through the old-students association in forty minutes, which frightened me a little.

Sudo Kaname was twenty-one. He lived in a flat two stations away, above a shop. He had been at a university in the city for one year and had left. He had a part-time job.

We went on a Saturday, all four of us, in the rain, and stood at the bottom of the stairs, and I understood on the way up that I had no plan at all.

He opened the door in a T-shirt with his hair not brushed, and he looked at us — a fourteen-year-old's complaint made flesh, a school president, a baseball player and a boy with a folder — and his whole face changed, and I have never in my life seen anybody look so caught.

"...You're from Kitami," he said.

"Yes," I said. "I'm the editor of the *Sakura Bulletin*."

And Sudo Kaname said, with a small terrible laugh: "It's still going?"

---

He let us in, which I had not expected. The flat was one room. There was no **fireplace**, no heating on, and it was very cold, and there were **piles** of things everywhere.

He did not **confess** straight away. First he was **contemptuous**, and then he was funny about it, and then he was angry, and then, quite quickly, he stopped.

"It was a joke," he said. "The list. It was a joke."

"It was not a joke to a first-year girl," I said, "who has not been in school for eleven days."

He looked at the floor.

I put the June issue on the table.

"Why did you write this?" I asked. "Three years ago. Why her?"

"I don't remember her," he said.

"Her name is Kurosawa Aya."

"I *don't remember her*." He said it too loudly. "That's — do you understand? That's the answer. I wrote a hundred of those. It took me four minutes each. I was seventeen and I was funny and eight hundred people read every word I wrote, and it was the only time in my whole life that has ever been true."

He put his hands over his face.

"They threw me out in my last term," he said, from behind them. "Three teachers signed a piece of paper. I didn't get the university reference. My mother — " He stopped. "You want to know why I did it now? Because in October my little cousin sent me a link and said *look, they put the old papers online*. And I opened it, and there were forty years of that paper, every issue, all of them, and eight months of them were *mine*, and I was the only person on earth who cared."

"So you copied it."

"So I copied it," he said. "And I made it worse, on purpose, because I wanted somebody to feel it."

Nobody said anything for a while. The rain went on outside.

Then Kurosawa, who had not spoken once, said, quietly:

"She is twenty-one as well."

Sudo did not look up.

"That is all I came to say," said Kurosawa, and went and stood by the door.

---

Sudo wrote it down. All of it, in his own hand, and signed it. He did it without being pushed, which is the only thing in this story I will give him.

Then he asked, at the door: "What are you going to print?"

And that was the question, wasn't it.

I could have printed everything. His name, his flat, the un-brushed hair, the cold room, the sentence about his mother. It would have been the biggest thing the *Sakura Bulletin* had ever run. It would have been true, every word, checked and signed, and I could have **dignified** it with the word *accountability*, and about six hundred people would have read it and enjoyed it very much.

I thought about a girl reading thirty-one words about herself in June.

"I'm going to print what was done," I said. "Not who did it."

Kenji stared at me. Even Kurosawa turned round.

"That's it?"

"The school will deal with you. The teachers will know your name and so will the old-students association, and that is a real consequence and it will follow you." I put the folder under my arm. "But four hundred students do not need your name. They need to know the paper was faked, that the three stories in it were false, and that the girl on page three did nothing. That's the useful part. The rest is just the bit that would be fun."

I went down the stairs into the rain.

Kurosawa caught up with me at the bottom.

"Aoki."

"I know. It's a smaller story."

"I was going to say," he said, "that Hoshino would have printed the same thing."

I had to stop walking for a second.

"No," I said. "She'd have printed it and then argued with me about it for an hour first. That's the part I miss."

---

### November 20th — The Review

Four pages. Five hundred copies. Out at seven in the morning on the day of the review.

The **heading** was: **THE PAPER OF NOVEMBER 2ND WAS NOT OURS. HERE IS WHAT HAPPENED.**

Page one: the **forgery**, how it was made, when, and how we know. The three false stories named as false, in plain language, one paragraph each. The first-year girl's story **negated** completely and directly, with the words *there is no truth in it whatsoever* in the largest type we had.

Page two: *The Whisper Page*, 2019 to 2020. Eight months. Thirty-one words in June. What a gossip column actually costs, written without a single joke in it.

Page three: our own **oversight**. That we put the archive online in October without thinking about who could use it. That the password to the school site was the founding year and had been for eleven years. That I was told this by a first-year and did not act.

Page four: an apology. Mine, signed, to Ms. Hayashi, to Tanabe Yuka, to a first-year student who is not named, and to Kurosawa Aya, who left this school three years ago and to whom this paper has owed nine words for a very long time.

I did not put Sudo's name anywhere.

The review was at four o'clock. It lasted six minutes.

Kurosawa read a statement he had written himself, proposing that **Clause** 6 be amended: any club that "performs a continuing school function" may operate with three members under permanent **advisory** status, subject to annual review. He proposed it as the author of the original **clause**. He did not explain why.

It passed, five votes to nothing.

Then, in the corridor afterwards, Yuka Tanabe said, "Aoki, put my name on the membership form," and when I asked why, she said, "Because you were fair to me in July when it cost you, and because I have decided I would rather be in the room where things get written down," and that was the whole of it.

Four members.

And on the twenty-first of November, at seven in the morning, Ryo Kurosawa came to the club room, put a form on the table, and said, "I do not want to discuss it, I am not going to write anything, and I will be leaving in March anyway."

I looked at the form for a while.

"You know this is going in the paper."

"I know," he said. "Print it small."

Five members.

We had done it. In eleven days, out of the worst thing that had ever happened to us, we had done the impossible thing.

I went home that night and sat on the stairs above the bakery and felt absolutely nothing at all, because the person I wanted to tell was not answering her phone.

## Chapter Ten — Departures

### December

She came back to school on the twenty-fourth of November.

She did not come back to the club.

For three weeks I watched Mizuki Hoshino sit two rows in front of me in class 2-B, hand in her work, answer when asked, and leave through the gate at four o'clock with her bag on her shoulder. She was not cold to me. That would have been easier. She said good morning. She lent me an eraser once. She was **considerate** in the way you are **considerate** to somebody you have met at a wedding.

I wrote her four messages and sent none of them. I am supposedly a writer. I could not construct one useful sentence.

"You're being a coward," Kenji said, on the fifteenth of December, eating my lunch.

"I know."

"You went to a man's flat and made him **confess** in writing. You stood up in front of six hundred people and asked a question. And you can't walk twelve metres across a classroom."

"That's different."

"It is not **remotely** different," said Kenji, "and you know it, which is why you look like that."

---

The frost came that week.

Every morning the field behind the **gymnasium** was white and hard, and the puddles by the gate had **frozen** into thin plates that first-years **stamped** on all the way to the door. The bakery **oven** became the best place in our town. My mother put a **tin** of biscuits on the counter for the postman, and the shop **glowed** yellow into a dark street at half past six every morning, and people came in just to stand in it for a minute.

On the twenty-third, **hail** came down for four minutes and stopped, and the school made a noise like a **circus**.

On the twenty-fourth, Yuka Tanabe put a piece of paper on my desk without stopping and kept walking.

It said: *She goes to the shrine on the hill on New Year's Eve with her father. Every year. It is the only tradition they have kept through six moves. She told me in October. I am not doing this for you, Aoki, I am doing it because she is my friend and she has been miserable for six weeks. — Y.T.*

---

### December 31st

Japanese people go to a shrine at New Year. It is called *hatsumode*. You go at midnight or in the first days of January, and you throw a coin, and you pray, and you buy a paper fortune, and you eat something hot from a stall while standing in the cold.

The shrine on the hill above our town is small. There is a stone path, a hundred and twelve steps, and two old **pine** trees at the top that lean towards each other.

It began to snow at half past eleven.

I stood at the top of the steps for forty minutes with my hands in my **pockets** and my breath going white and snow in my hair and **stockings** would have been an excellent idea, and I watched about two hundred people come up those steps, and none of them was her.

At six minutes past midnight, she came up the steps with her father.

She saw me. She stopped, four steps from the top.

Her father, who I think had known for some time exactly what was happening, said, "I am going to look at the fortunes," and walked away with the total lack of **fuss** that I have decided is the best thing about that man.

We stood in the snow while the bell was ringing.

"Aoki."

"I have a thing to say," I said, "and I've written it eleven times and they were all terrible, so I'm going to say the ugly version."

"Alright."

I made myself look at her.

"In that room, they asked me if I knew about Tokyo, and I said no. And you told me the problem wasn't the lie. You were right, and it took me about a day to understand what you actually meant, and then it took me six weeks to be brave enough to say it out loud." Snow was landing on her shoulders and not melting. "The problem is that in that moment, some part of me was **ashamed** of you. Not for long. Two seconds. But it was there, and you heard it, and I have been telling myself since November that I was tired and frightened and it was a mistake, and it was — but it was also true for two seconds, and I have to say that part or the apology is worth nothing."

The bell rang again. Somebody down the hill laughed at something.

"I was **ashamed** of you," I said, "and there was nothing to be **ashamed** of. You did the bravest thing anybody in this story has done, and you did it when you were fifteen, and you paid for it, and I know all of that, and I *still* flinched. So I'm sorry. Not for lying. For flinching."

Mizuki did not say anything for a long time.

Then she came up the last four steps, so that she was standing on the same level as me, which she had to do on purpose.

"Do you know what I have been doing for six weeks?" she said.

"No."

"Reading your November issue." She pushed her hair out of her face. "Four pages. You did not print his name. Everybody wanted you to and you did not, and you put your own **oversight** on page three where everybody could see it, and you apologised to a girl who left this school three years ago and who you have never met."

"That was the right thing to print."

"Yes," she said. "It was. And I sat in my room with it and I thought: *the boy who wrote this is not somebody who thinks I am a stain.*" Her voice was not steady now. "And then I thought: *so why won't he come and say it?* And then it was three weeks, and then it was five, and I was so angry at you, Aoki, for making me be the one who was waiting."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"You are extremely **clumsy**."

"I know."

"I forgave you in about eight days," she said. "The other five weeks were **spite**."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half a laugh and half something else, and she looked up at me, and there was snow on her eyelashes, and I have never in my life wanted so badly to be a person who says the right thing at the right speed.

"Hoshino—"

"Mizuki," she said.

"...What?"

"It's been nine months," said Mizuki Hoshino. "Say the other one."

So I did.

And then, because I am who I am, I said the actual sentence too — the whole ugly **straightforward** thing, no metaphor, no clever ending, just the plain fact, standing in the snow at the top of a hundred and twelve steps.

She listened to all of it with her chin up.

Then she said, "Good," and took hold of the front of my coat with both hands, and pulled, and the bell rang for the last time somewhere below us, and it was, without any **contest** whatsoever, the best New Year of my life.

---

### January

My grandmother's operation was on the fourteenth.

It took two hours and forty minutes. We sat in the **corridor** outside the **ward** on plastic chairs — my mother, my father, me — and my father did not speak once for the entire time, and at the end of it he put one hand on the top of my head for about three seconds, which in the language of Aoki men is roughly a symphony.

She was awake by the evening and complaining about the food by the following **midday**.

"It was the size of a **pea**," she said, with enormous **contempt**, from the bed. "Four days in here. For a **pea**."

"Grandmother."

"And the cost, Sota. Do you know what the cost is?"

"Don't tell me."

"I will tell everybody," she said. "It is a **splendid** story. I am going to bore this entire hospital with it."

Mizuki came on the Saturday, on the train, by herself, with a bag of oranges and a small notebook.

My grandmother took one look at her and said, "Ah. The one with the mask."

"That was September."

"I know exactly when it was." She patted the bed. "Sit. Tell me about the **calf**."

They talked for fifty minutes. I was sent to get tea and came back to find them **deep** in a conversation about the price of milk in which I had no part whatsoever, and my grandmother — who has never once in her life said something kind about somebody in front of them — said to me, over Mizuki's head:

"This one is **resourceful**. Keep her near the family."

"*Grandmother.*"

On the train home Mizuki was quiet for a long time, and then she said: "Her hands."

"I know."

"There are more **wrinkles** on the back of one of her hands than there are lines on this page." She looked out of the window at the dark. "She **patted** my arm when I left. Twice. Like a person checking a horse."

"That's her whole emotional range. You got the top of it."

"I know," said Mizuki. "That is why I am going to **cherish** it."

The doctor used the word *routine*. I have decided that is the most beautiful word in medicine. Nobody ever calls it a **miracle**, and I do not think it was one — it was one appointment in September that she nearly did not make, and a machine, and a woman with steady hands on the fourteenth of January. But I sat on a plastic chair for two hours and forty minutes with my whole chest full of a horrible bright **expectancy**, and when the door opened and somebody said *it went well*, I understood what people mean by **fulfilment**, which up to that point I had thought was a word for advertisements.

I **marvel** at it still, in the plain way. She is eighty now. She sends me photographs of the **calf**, which is not a **calf** any more.

---

### February

The third-years took their entrance exams.

The whole school went quiet in a way it never is at any other time. The library filled up at seven in the morning. The **strain** showed on people's faces in the corridor. Kenji, who was one year below all of it and could not have cared less, walked around whistling until three separate third-years threatened his life.

Kurosawa was accepted by a national university, to study public administration and law. He told nobody. I found out because the **clerk** in the school office told my mother in the bakery, which is how everything in that town has always worked.

I asked him about it in the club room.

"You are going to write policy for the rest of your life," I said.

"Yes."

"Rules. For other people."

"Yes," said Kurosawa. He was straightening the chairs, which he does whenever he is in a room, without noticing. "I intend to be extremely careful about it."

"Why law, though?"

He thought about it for a while.

"Because a rule can do everything a rumour does," he said, "except that a rule is written down, and a written thing can be argued with." He pushed the last chair in. "I would rather be argued with than obeyed, Aoki. I did not understand that when I was fourteen."

---

### The Last Week of February

She told me on the Tuesday, in the club room, after everybody else had gone.

Her father's **corporation** was moving him again. Singapore this time. He would go in April, and this time — because she was going into her third year, and because the timing was what it was — she would go with him.

"They arranged it in January," she said. "He tried to refuse. He has never once tried to refuse before, and he did it for me, and they said no, and he came home and could not look at me for two days."

I sat down on the **stool**.

"When?"

"The twenty-eighth of March."

"That's five weeks."

"That's five weeks," she agreed.

Neither of us said anything clever. There was nothing clever. That is the thing nobody tells you about the actual shape of these events: they arrive as a date, and a **departure** time, and a flight number, and there is no argument to have with any of it.

"I am not going to do the thing," she said at last.

"What thing?"

"The thing where I become sad in advance." She opened her notebook and put it on the table with a small hard sound. "We have five weeks and one issue of the paper left. I would like to spend the five weeks and make the issue. That is my **resolution** and I am not taking questions."

---

### March

The graduation ceremony is on the first of March. It is **solemn**, and slow, and everybody cries, including several people who spent three years claiming they hated the place.

The third-years sat in rows in the **auditorium** in their uniforms. The **chorus** sang. The headmaster spoke for eleven minutes. Each student walked up and received a **certificate** with a small bow.

When Kurosawa's name was called, four people at the back **clapped** before the polite general applause started: me, Kenji, Yuka, and Mizuki.

He did not turn around. But the back of his neck went red, and I am going to hold on to that for the rest of my life.

Afterwards, in the corridor, with the **ceremonial** flowers still in his hand, he said:

"The **advisory** status is permanent now. It cannot be removed by a council vote. I made sure of that in January."

"I know. I read the minutes."

"Of course you did." He almost smiled. "Aoki. Print small things carefully. That is the whole job."

Then he shook my hand, which was the first and last time he ever did that, and walked out of the gate into the rest of his life.

---

The last issue of the year came out on the twenty-fifth of March. Twelve pages. Six hundred copies. Every one of them gone by the end of the day.

We made it in eleven days, the five of us, in a room with a flickering **bulb** and a 1955 **typewriter** on the **cabinet** and four buckets in the corner from the last time it rained.

I have put it at the end of this book, because it is the best thing we ever made, and because it is easier to show you than to describe.

---

### March 28th — The Airport

They live two hours from us, so we took the train, and then the **shuttle** bus from the station along the **expressway** to the terminal.

Kenji came. Yuka came. Ms. Hayashi came, which surprised everybody, and drank an airport coffee and complained about the price of it for eleven minutes.

The **departure** hall was full of people saying goodbye badly, which is the only way anybody says it.

There is a particular **sorrow** in an airport that does not exist anywhere else, and it is caused by the furniture. Everything in that building is designed to move people along: the moving walkway, the rows of seats bolted in place, the trolleys, the signs. Nothing in it is built for standing still, and standing still is the only thing anybody there actually wants to do.

Two families near us were doing it much worse than we were. One was a group of about nine seeing off a young man in a suit, all talking at once in **haste**, none of them saying the real thing. The other was a woman with two small children and eleven bags, an **immigrant** family going home or arriving, I could not tell which, and the smaller child was chewing **gum** and **hanging** off her coat and had clearly decided that if she **clung** hard enough the whole day could be **bypassed**.

I understood her completely.

Kenji, to his eternal credit, did not make one single joke. He shook her father's hand, said "Thank you for the year," which he had obviously practised, and then went and stood by a **pillar** and looked at the ceiling for four minutes.

Her father shook my hand and said, "Thank you for this year," and then went to deal with the **freight** charges on the extra bag, because Mizuki had insisted on taking six kilos of newspapers.

We stood by the window. You could see the runway from there, and a plane going out every three or four minutes, rolling, then **halting**, then **thrusting** forward and lifting.

"Right," said Mizuki. "The **arrangement**."

"The **arrangement**."

She held up one finger. "Every month, I send you a column. Nine hundred words. From Singapore. About whatever I find. You print it, and you do not cut it without asking me first."

"Agreed."

"Second." Second finger. "You send me every issue. In an envelope. On paper. I do not want a file, Aoki, I want the grey paper from Mr. Ito with the wrong margins."

"Agreed."

"Third." She stopped.

The third one took her a while.

"Third," she said. "In August, next year, I have two weeks. And I would like to spend them at a farm two hours from here where there is a **bull** the size of a car."

"...She'll make you pull weeds."

"I know," said Mizuki. "I have thought about it constantly since August."

Then she took the black notebook out of her bag — the soft-cornered one, the one she had carried every day since April and never opened in front of anyone — and put it in my hands.

"Don't read it on the train," she said. "Read it in the room."

"Mizuki—"

"I'll be back," she said. "That is not a brave thing to say. It is a **flight** that takes seven hours and I have looked up the price and it is not even that much."

Then she hugged me, hard and briefly, in front of my best friend, my class representative, and my English teacher, and went through the gate without turning around, because she is the strongest person I have ever met and because if she had turned around it would have been over.

---

### April, Again

The plane took off at four twenty. We watched it go from the window: the long **roll**, the **takeoff**, the **jet** noise coming a second late through the glass, and then a small white shape climbing over the bay and going out **overseas**, until there was nothing.

I read the notebook that night, in the club room, alone.

It was full of writing. All of it hers. It started in Tokyo eighteen months before, three days after she stood in front of a hall and read somebody else's apology, and the first page said:

*I am going to stop writing. This is the last thing.*

And then there were four hundred more pages, because she had never stopped, not for one single week, she had only stopped letting anybody see it.

On the last written page, dated the twenty-fourth of March, it said:

*Kitami High School. Newspaper club. Five members.*
*This is the first place I have ever been where the thing I am was not a problem to be managed.*
*Sota — if you are reading this, you got to the end. Of course you did. You always read to the end. That is the entire reason I like you.*

---

The cherry trees on the **lane** came out in the second week of April.

They open, everybody takes photographs, and eight days later a **breeze** blows them all away. Every year it is the same, and every year it is completely different, which is a thing I did not understand until I was seventeen.

I am editor now. There are five names on the membership form on the wall, and one of them lives four thousand kilometres away and sends nine hundred words on the first of every month, and has never once been late.

The room is still at the end of the third-floor hall, past the science room, where nobody walks.

There is still a **mop** in the corner.

I have decided never to move it.

### PAGE ONE — FROM THE EDITOR

This is the last issue of the year, and I am **contractually** obliged to be sincere in it, so here we go.

In April this club had one member. On the twenty-fifth of March it has five. In between we printed eleven issues, sold four hundred and eighty coffees, lost a **confrontation** with the student council and then, in a way, won it, and had our name stolen and given back.

I would like to say something about the word **accomplishment**.

I do not think ours was the sixteen-page festival special, although it is the thing everybody mentions. I think our real **accomplishment** was the four ugly pages of the twentieth of November, in which this paper wrote down its own **oversight** in public, and did not enjoy itself, and printed no name that did not need printing.

Anybody can be brave in a good week. **Integrity** is what is left over in a bad one.

To the third-years leaving us: this is your **commencement**, which is a strange English word, because it means both an ending ceremony and a beginning. **Forth** you go, **onward**, into a **generation** that will complain about you within four years. To the first-years arriving in April: the club room is at the end of the third-floor hall. There is a kettle in it.

I have been told by three separate people that this letter is too **heartfelt**. I have decided to **renew** my **commitment** to being **heartfelt** anyway. It is the last issue. I can be sincere once a year.

**S. Aoki (2nd year), Editor**

### PAGE THREE — SCIENCE CORNER

*by K. Sasaki (1st year)*

People keep asking me what I actually do, so: I look after the school website, and this term I have been reading about the sky.

The **ozone** layer is a thin band of gas about twenty-five kilometres above your head, and it stops most of the sun's worst light from reaching you. In the 1980s it was **deteriorating** badly because of certain gases used in fridges and **spray** cans. Countries agreed to stop making them. It is now slowly repairing itself. This is worth knowing, because it is the clearest **proven** example we have of the whole world **conforming** to one rule and it actually working.

Other things I learned this term, in no order:

**Geology** is the study of what the ground is made of. Under this town there is soft rock and a great deal of clay. Our school is built on a slight **mount**, which is why the west wing has a step in the middle of a corridor for no visible reason.

**Petroleum** is not one substance. It is a mixture, and refining it means separating that mixture at different temperatures — some parts come off at 40 degrees **centigrade**, some at 350.

An **organism** is any living thing, from a whale to the **mould** on the wall of the wooden store. A **colony** is a group of them living together. **Bacteria** in a **colony** behave differently from **bacteria** alone, which I find slightly disturbing.

The **minerals** in ordinary tap water in this town include calcium and magnesium, which is why the club room kettle has a white layer inside it that nobody has ever cleaned.

A **variation** of one degree in average temperature does not sound like much. It is not a **trifle**. Small numbers across a large system are the whole story.

Finally: birds **glide** rather than flap whenever they can, because flapping is expensive. A crow coming down from the **summit** of the school roof to the **oval** flowerbed will **descend** most of the way without moving its wings at all. Watch one. That **descent** is free.

### PAGE FIVE — THE DEBATE CLUB'S YEAR

*by the debate club, edited (**sparingly**) by M. Hoshino*

This year's motions and results, for the record.

**"This house would ban mobile phones in school."** Defeated. The **opposition** argued that the rule already exists and is ignored, and that a rule which cannot be enforced makes all other rules weaker. **Contention** of the evening, from a first-year: "You cannot **criminalize** a thing four hundred people do every day and call it **discipline**."

**"This house believes a school should have a written constitution."** Passed easily, which surprised us, since ours has one and nobody reads it. The winning speech pointed out that a written rule can be argued with and an unwritten **convention** cannot.

**"This house believes talent is **attributable** mostly to practice."** Very close. The **contrary** view — that we are far less in control of ourselves than we like to believe — was argued with **profundity** by a second-year who then admitted afterwards that she did not believe a word of it. That is **advisable** practice in debating and a terrible habit in life.

**"This house would abolish school uniform."** Defeated, 31–24. The **foremost** argument for keeping it was not tradition. It was money: without uniform, what you wear becomes visible information about your family, and a school where the **affluent** and the **middle-class** and everybody else are instantly sorted by clothing is not a school anybody should want. Nobody had a good answer. The **implicit** point — that uniform hides a real difference rather than removing it — was made and not resolved.

**"This house believes **democratic** government is the least bad system."** Passed, 44–7, which the club felt was too easy a win to be useful, so they ran it again in February with the sides swapped and made everybody argue the position they disagreed with. That second debate was better. The **supposition** underneath the motion — that there is one **supreme** system that fits every **continent** and every group of people who **inhabit** one — was **confronted** properly for the first time. Somebody quoted a **communist** thinker; somebody else pointed out that a small farming **commune** and a **federation** of two hundred million people are not solving the same problem. Nobody's mind was changed. Everybody's argument improved.

**"This house believes school rules should be more **permissive**."** Defeated narrowly. The interesting part was not the vote. It was that both sides used the word *freedom* to mean completely different things: one side meant **liberty** from being told what to do, the other meant being free to work without **interference**. Half of all disagreement, our club president says, is two people **purposefully** using one word for two things.

**"This house believes exam results are a **gross** measure of a person."** Passed unanimously, and was therefore a bad debate, and we are not running motions like that again.

**A note on **advocacy**.** Three times this year, students came to us wanting the club to argue *for* something in public — a cause they cared about. We said no each time, and it made us unpopular, so here is the reasoning. A debate club exists to practise **confronting** an argument, not to **impose** one. **Advocacy** is a different and honourable activity and it belongs somewhere else in the building.

**The motion we refused to run.** In November, somebody submitted a motion about a group of people in this town. It was **discriminatory** in its wording, and we did not print it or debate it, and we told the person why to his face. **Prejudice** does not become an argument by being written in the form of one. This is the only time all year the club has **prevailed** on a question of what *not* to discuss, and we would do it again.

**Smaller things we learned.** That the word *obviously* is usually a sign that a speaker has run out of reasons. That **contemporaneous** evidence — written at the time — beats a memory every time. That in any argument about a **famine** or a **plague** or any large **catastrophe**, the **implication** that somebody could simply have fixed it is nearly always **incidental** to what actually happened. That claims of **superiority** are the easiest thing in the world to make and the hardest thing in the world to support with anything **tangible**.

**A note on how we argue.** Twice this year a debate collapsed into people being unpleasant to each other. Both times it was **regarding** a motion where somebody in the room had a personal stake. We have made a rule about it: you may attack an argument as much as you like, but **lest** anybody forget, the person holding it goes home to a house.

**Words we banned from our own room this year,** after using them badly: *obviously*, *everyone knows*, and *you would say that*. All three are ways of avoiding an argument while appearing to win one.

We meet on Thursdays. New members welcome, **regardless** of experience.

### PAGE SIX — THE DRAMA CLUB: *THE LAST WINTER OF LORD SANADA*

*A review, by S. Aoki*

I went to two rehearsals in October expecting to be kind about a **comedy** of errors, and instead I saw the best thing performed in this building in my lifetime. This is a spoiler-free review, mostly, so I will keep the **spoiler** to one sentence at the end and mark it clearly.

The play covers eleven months of a **siege**. The castle holds; the **invasion** comes anyway; and when the enemy finally **invade**, they are not monsters, which is the whole point.

What the club got right was scale. A cast of nineteen played an army. The **armament** was painted wood. There were no **blades** on stage that could cut butter. And yet the second-act **raid** — the **blast** of the drum, the sudden **boom** of the whole cast stamping once, together — made six hundred people move backwards in their seats.

What it got right more importantly was the **civilians**. Most stories about **warfare** are about the people who chose it. This one kept returning to a farmer's family, three **veterans** too old to fight, and a **lass** of about twelve who spends the entire play trying to keep a goat alive. She has fourteen lines. She is the best character in it.

There is no **brutality** shown on stage. There is a great deal of it described, quietly, by people who have come back from somewhere, and it is far worse. The word **butchery** is used exactly once, in the fourth act, by a man who then apologises for using it.

The **noble** family is played straight and never made **ridiculous**. Lord Sanada's daughter — betrothed as a **bride** to a family she has met twice, and quite clear-eyed about what that is — refuses both the **fantasy** of rescue and the **fantasy** of **sacrificial** death. Her line in Act Five ("I will **wed** him, and I will be **wary**, and I will outlive all of you") got the loudest sound of the night.

The ending is not a **triumph** and it is not **doom**. It is a treaty. People are **liberated** from a **siege** and go home to a burned valley and start again, and the last image is a woman **kneeling** in a field pulling **weeds**.

I have thought about that field all winter.

**Three more things, briefly.**

The **staging** was the most **accomplished** thing about it. There is no stage machinery in our **auditorium** — no turning floor, no lift — and the club solved the whole **spatial** problem with eleven wooden boxes and the **discipline** to move them in the dark without a sound. The castle, the **wilderness** outside it, and the valley afterwards were all the same eleven boxes.

The acting was, with two exceptions, unshowy. The two exceptions were the messenger in Act Two, who was so **demonstrative** that people laughed in the wrong place, and Lord Sanada himself, who was slightly too **mannered** in the early scenes and then, from Act Three onwards, extremely good. Nobody **whipped** the audience; nobody **clawed** at the emotion. They trusted a quiet line to work, and it did.

And the play refuses the easy shape. There is no **rescuer**. Nobody arrives on a horse. The **princess** does not need one, the **orphan** boy in Act Four is not adopted by anybody, and the enemy general is never **overthrown** — he simply gets old and goes home. When the **invading** army finally comes over the hill, there is no **rousing** music at all; there is one drum, and the whole cast standing still, and a small **frightened** sound from the goat.

I watched a row of second-years in front of me, and every one of them had their **brows** down, leaning forward. Nobody moved for the last eleven minutes.

That is a **clear-cut** success and this school should be **proud** of them.

**SPOILER (skip this line if you are seeing the March re-run):** *the goat lives.*

### PAGE TEN — THE BAKERY COLUMN

*Six things I know after twenty-six years behind a counter, by A. Aoki (mother of the editor, who has not been allowed to edit this)*

**One.** Bread goes **stale** because water leaves it, not because it dries out in the way you think. A **loaf** kept in the fridge goes **stale** faster than one on the counter. Everybody in this town does it wrong and I have stopped telling them.

**Two.** A **staple** food is one people eat every day without thinking. Rice here, bread in other places. The test of a **staple** is not that it is delicious. It is that nobody notices it until it is missing.

**Three.** **Processed** food is not a moral category. Bread is **processed**. Cheese is **processed**. Everything you eat has been through a **process** except an apple, and somebody grew that on purpose. Be suspicious of anybody who uses the word as an insult and cannot tell you what they mean.

**Four.** If you **chop** an onion and it makes you cry, your knife is not sharp enough. A sharp knife breaks fewer cells and releases less of the gas that causes the **odour** and the tears. This is the single most useful thing in this column.

**Five.** I sell approximately one **pint** of coffee an hour, ten hours a day, and I know what three hundred people in this town are worried about, because people tell a **cashier** things. I have never repeated any of it. This is why the shop still exists. A small business runs on **discretion**, not on bread.

**Six.** People say a bakery is a lovely job. It is a good job. It starts at four in the morning, my back hurts, and in a bad month we are entirely at the mercy of the price of flour. But we have fed this street for twenty-six years, and my son has grown up understanding that things do not simply appear, and that is worth more than a **champagne** life. My husband would like me to add that the **liquor** shop next door has been offering him **brandy** at cost price since 1998 and he has never once accepted it. He is very proud of this and I am contractually required to mention it.

**Recipe of the month:** toss stale bread cubes in oil, salt and garlic, bake at 180°C for ten minutes, and you have croutons. Nothing in a bakery is ever thrown away. Nothing.

### PAGE ELEVEN — THE FIRST FOREIGN COLUMN

*Mizuki Hoshino leaves for Singapore on 28 March and will write nine hundred words for this paper on the first of every month. Below is an early piece, written the week before departure.*

---

I have moved six times and I have learned one useful thing about arriving somewhere: you do not get to choose what you notice first, and what you notice first will be wrong.

The first time we moved, I was seven. I noticed the **pedestrian** crossings, because they sang. Every crossing in that city played a small tune. I decided the whole place was cheerful, which it was not.

The second time, I noticed the **metro**, and then the **avenue** outside the station, and then the eight-lane **highway** beyond it, and I decided the city was **inhuman**, which it also was not.

What I have finally understood, at seventeen, is that **familiarity** is not something a place gives you. It is something you build, slowly, from very small things: the shop that is open late, the one bus driver who nods, the corner where the wind is always wrong.

You cannot **acquaint** yourself with a city. You can only start.

So this is my plan for Singapore, and I am writing it down here so that six hundred people can hold me to it.

I will not stay in the **abstracted** part of it — the airport, the mall, the **hotel** version of a country that is the same everywhere and is a kind of **artificiality** with air conditioning. That version is easy and it is **illusory** and you can live in it for two years and learn nothing.

Instead: I am going to find a market and go every week until somebody knows my face. I am going to learn what a **banknote** feels like there and what the **coinage** is, and stop converting prices in my head, because as long as you are converting you are still a **consumer** and not an **inhabitant**. I am going to take one **excursion** a month to somewhere I have not read about first. I am going to be **wary** of any opinion I form in the first sixty days.

And I am going to write it down, **invariably**, on the first of the month, even in the months when nothing happens — *especially* in those months, because a place is what it is on an ordinary Tuesday, not on the day something interesting occurs.

People keep telling me this is a great **venture**. It is not. It is my father's job and a suitcase.

But here is the thing I did not know a year ago, and which is the **sole** reason I am able to get on the plane at all.

I used to think that moving meant losing the place you left. That it went out like a light behind you; that after a year the people there would be, at best, an **acquaintance**; that there was no point in building anything because you would only have to leave it.

Then this year I helped make eleven issues of a small grey newspaper in a room with a leaking ceiling, and I discovered that the things you write down do not stay behind you. They are the one part of a place that travels.

I am not losing Kitami. I have a **column** in it.

*— M. Hoshino*

**MS. HAYASHI'S LAST ENGLISH NOTE OF THE YEAR**

*Every issue this year I have been given a small box at the back and told to be educational. This is the last one. I am going to spend it on words that hide something.*

**Mr. and Mrs.** — "Mr." is a worn-down spelling of **mister**, which is a worn-down form of *master*. "Mrs." is a worn-down spelling of **mistress**, which once simply meant the female head of a household, and which English then **manipulated** into something else entirely, so that today a **spouse** and a **mistress** are opposites. One word, four hundred years, and a whole social history you can see if you look. Nothing in a language is an accident.

**Freeway** — the "free" does not mean free of charge. It means free of crossings and traffic lights. Americans say **freeway**, we say expressway, the British say motorway, and all three are the same road.

**Dime** — a US ten-cent coin, from a Latin word meaning *tenth*. If somebody says a thing is "a dime a dozen," they mean it is worth almost nothing because there is a great deal of it.

**Bail** — money left with a court so that an accused person can go home before the trial. Notice that it is not a punishment and not a proof of anything. You will hear this word wrongly used on television all year.

**Estate** — land and buildings that somebody owns. In Britain it also means a group of houses built together, which confuses everybody.

**Well-off** — having enough money. Politer than "rich," which is why people who are rich prefer it.

**Carriage** — a vehicle pulled by horses, *and* the way a person holds their body. Same word, and if you think about it for eight seconds you will see why.

**Recreation** — your free-time activity. Look at the middle of it: *re-creation*. English is telling you what rest is for.

**Renewal, anew, forthcoming, inward, outward, overhead, besides.** These seven are all doors. A learner who knows *new, in, out, over, side* already owns most of them, and I would like more of you to guess before reaching for a dictionary. Guessing is not cheating. It is reading.

**Overhead** deserves a second line: it means "above your head," and in business it means the costs you carry whether or not you sell anything — the rent, the lights. The same picture in both.

**Voluminous** — very large. Usually of a book, a skirt, or a report nobody will read.

**Literary** means "relating to literature." It is not a compliment by itself. A **literary** style can be beautiful or it can be a way of **hindering** the reader while appearing clever. Our editor spent this year removing his own **literary** sentences and the paper improved every single time.

**Wit** is not jokes. **Wit** is speed. A person with **wit** puts two ordinary things together faster than you expected.

**Proverbial** means "the one everybody has heard of," because it appears in a proverb. If somebody mentions "the **proverbial** last straw," they are pointing at a saying without finishing it, and expecting you to know. You are allowed to say you do not.

**Behavioural** — relating to how people actually act, rather than what they say. A **behavioural** study watches. This is also the whole method of this newspaper.

**Principled** — acting from a rule you have decided on, even when it costs you. Our editor turned down the biggest story of his year in November on a point of principle, and I am not going to say more than that, because he will go red.

**Convey** — to carry something from one place to another, and also to get a meaning across. Both senses are the same picture. When your English teacher writes *what are you trying to convey here?* in the margin, she means: this sentence has not arrived.

**Three verbs of force, which learners confuse.** To **pierce** is to make a hole by pushing something sharp through. To **plunge** is to go down suddenly and completely — into water, or a price falling. To **spit** is to force liquid out of the mouth, and it is also what a hot pan does with oil. All three are short, hard, Anglo-Saxon words, and English keeps its short words for its physical ones.

**Intrusion** — the noun of *intrude*. Somebody entering where they were not invited. It is a colder word than *interruption* and you should use it carefully, because it accuses.

**A note on spelling.** The growth on the wall of the old wooden store is **mould** in Britain and **mold** in America. So are *colour/color*, *harbour/harbor*, *tyre/tire*, *gaol/jail*. Both are correct. Choose one, be consistent for the length of a piece of writing, and stop worrying about it — which is advice I give every April and which nobody takes.

**Ridicule, envy, intrude, impress.** Four verbs that describe what people do to each other in a school corridor. Learn all four. You will need them this week.

**Spokesman, commentator, pollster.** Three jobs that sound similar and are not. A **spokesman** speaks *for* somebody and is paid to. A **commentator** speaks *about* things and is paid for opinions. A **pollster** asks a large number of people a careful question and reports the numbers, and is the only one of the three whose work can be checked. When you read the news, always ask which of the three you are listening to.

**Proceedings** — the official written record of a meeting. Boring, and the most powerful document in any organisation, because in five years it is the only thing left.

**Finally: a word I want back.** **Conscientiousness.** It is long and ugly and it will never be fashionable. It means doing the work properly when nobody is checking. Every good thing in this paper this year was produced by it, and by five children who kept turning up. If your English **resembles** mine at forty, you will have forgotten half your vocabulary and remembered which words mattered. That is one of them.

*Have a good spring. Do not look up every word. — S. Hayashi*
